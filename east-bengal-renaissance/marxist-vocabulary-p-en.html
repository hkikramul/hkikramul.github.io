Marxist vocabulary · Part two

Selected terms beginning with P

This installment explains 22 entries from the P section of the Marxism Glossary at the Marxists Internet Archive in plain language, with Bangladesh-focused illustrations. Some terms belong directly to Marxian political economy; others come from Hegel, Sartre, Lenin, Luxemburg or different philosophical traditions. They should not all be treated as Marx’s own terms. The Bangladesh examples are illustrative, not verified descriptions of particular institutions or events.

Theory, practice and philosophy

Practical Idea

This is a technical term in Hegel’s philosophy, not an everyday phrase meaning “an idea that works.” Hegel uses it to discuss the relationship between knowledge, will and reality. It is best read as part of the philosophical background to Marx’s thought, not as a concept coined by Marx. In discussions of policy in Bangladesh, one can examine the relation between theory, decisions and real outcomes without applying this term directly.

Practice and Theory

In Marxist discussions, practice is more than simply doing something. It includes knowledge, planning, organisation and material activity through which people change their world. Theory learns from lived experience, while theory helps people understand the aims and methods of action. A wage proposal in Bangladesh, for example, needs more than policy language: it should consider workers’ experience, living costs and wage evidence, then be revised in light of its results.

Practico-inert

Jean-Paul Sartre used this term for structures created through collective human activity that later become rigid and stop responding to people’s needs, sometimes obstructing the very people who created them. Imagine an organisation formed to support workers whose procedures and administrative layers eventually prevent workers’ complaints from being heard. This is an illustrative use of Sartre’s concept, not a claim about a specific Bangladeshi institution or a description of all bureaucracy.

Pragmatism

Pragmatism is a distinct philosophical tradition associated with thinkers such as Peirce, William James and John Dewey. It asks how concepts gain meaning through practical consequences and experience. Pragmatism and Marxism have points of contact, but they are not identical. Marxist analysis generally places human activity within social relations, history and structures of power. When evaluating a policy in Bangladesh, it matters to examine outcomes, but also who benefits and who bears the costs.

Praxis

Praxis refers to theory-informed practice, with theory and action understood as connected rather than fully separable. The term became especially prominent in later Marxist discussions, though Marx used it in a manuscript from 1844. Research, organising, negotiation and learning from experience can be viewed together as praxis in work on labour rights in Bangladesh.

Value, production and property

Price

Price is the monetary amount at which a good or service is exchanged. In Marxian political economy, price and value are not identical: market prices can fluctuate, and understanding the production, labour and social relations behind them requires further analysis. The export price of a Bangladeshi garment alone does not tell us workers’ wages, the value created in production or how gains are distributed through the supply chain.

Primitive accumulation

Marx used this concept for historical processes that helped establish capitalism by separating large populations from land, tools and other means of livelihood, pushing them toward wage labour. It is not simply a story of peaceful saving or personal thrift; dispossession and coercion are central to the analysis. The concept can prompt questions about land acquisition, changing rural livelihoods or urban expansion in Bangladesh: who gains from the change, and who bears the loss of a livelihood? Each case requires its own evidence.

Private labour

Private labour is work organised on the account of individuals or private enterprises, whose social usefulness and recognition may become visible only through exchange in the market. Under capitalism, production can be collective while ownership and decision-making remain in private hands. The concept invites questions about who makes decisions and whose labour is recognised through the market, whether in small-scale production or export-oriented factories in Bangladesh.

Private property

In everyday use, private property can include people’s personal belongings. Marxist critique usually focuses on ownership of the means of production, such as land, factories, machinery and capital, which can give owners control over other people’s labour. Personal possessions and ownership of productive assets should not be conflated. Ownership of a factory, for instance, is connected to who controls work and the results of production.

Privatisation and nationalisation

Privatisation transfers ownership or management of an enterprise to private hands; nationalisation transfers it to the state. A change in ownership alone does not guarantee workers’ or the public’s participation in decisions. Assessing a Bangladeshi state-owned or private enterprise requires asking who it is accountable to, who receives its services, what protections workers have and how the public interest is served. “State-owned” does not automatically mean worker-controlled or public-serving.

Production and consumption

Production and consumption are distinct but connected. What gets produced relates to people’s needs and purchasing power, while the organisation of production affects work, income and opportunities to consume. Bangladesh’s garment industry produces for domestic and international markets; workers’ income also shapes their ability to meet their own needs. Understanding the relationship requires looking beyond output to wages, working conditions and purchasing power.

Productive forces

Productive forces include human labour and skill, machinery, technology, knowledge, infrastructure and resources drawn from nature. How these are used also depends on ownership and the organisation of work. In Bangladesh’s garment industry, workers’ skills, machinery, electricity and transport all contribute to production. Growth in productive capacity does not guarantee higher wages or safer work; how gains are distributed matters too.

Productive and unproductive labour

In Marxian political economy, “productive” here does not mean socially useful. It refers to whether labour produces surplus value for capital. Marx used a teacher employed by a private, profit-making school as an example of productive labour; the same educational work in a state institution does not stand in the same relation to capital. This distinction does not diminish the social contribution of teachers, health workers, caregivers or public employees.

Class, profit and political organisation

Presidium

A presidium can be a standing committee authorised to act for a larger body when it is not in session. If a union, cooperative or political organisation uses one, its authority, accountability to members and reporting to the full assembly matter. The term describes an organisational structure; it does not by itself guarantee democracy.

Professional revolutionary

In Leninist discussions, this can mean a member or organiser who devotes much of their available time to revolutionary political work. In Lenin’s context, “professional” did not simply mean a salaried party official. Historical debates also raise the risk that a leadership layer could become separated from ordinary people. Applying the term to a Bangladeshi organisation would require examining members’ actual roles and accountability.

Professionalisation

Professionalisation occurs when an activity once done informally within a family or community becomes a paid profession or industry. It can bring training, standards and livelihoods to areas such as sport or care work, but it may also raise the cost of services and narrow the space for mutual support. Both sides matter when considering care, coaching or cultural work in Bangladesh.

Profit

In accounting, profit is generally what remains after expenses are deducted from revenue. Marxian analysis connects profit to surplus value created in production and its distribution; interest, rent, taxes, commercial margins and competition can shape that distribution. For export production in Bangladesh, sales revenue alone cannot establish workers’ wages, profit or the share of surplus; costs and supply-chain information are also needed.

Proletariat

In Marxist theory, the proletariat is a class whose livelihood depends primarily on selling its labour-power and which does not receive profit as an owner of productive capital. It is not simply a synonym for “poor people” or a particular income bracket. People who depend on wages across factories, construction, transport, retail, services and informal work in Bangladesh have different experiences; class position also involves working conditions, ownership, bargaining power and alternatives for making a living.

Proletarian democracy

The meaning and practice of socialist democracy are contested within socialist traditions. One interpretation centres the organised participation of working people, equality and influence over public decisions; Rosa Luxemburg’s writing stresses political participation and public criticism. Historically, the language of “rule by the working class” has also been used to justify one-party power or suppress opposition. It should therefore be assessed by actual rights to speak, organise, elect, hold leaders accountable and influence decisions.

Property

For Marx and Engels, property is not only a question of whose name appears on an object; control over resources also shapes relationships among people and the distribution of labour. Ownership of land, factories or machinery can determine who makes production decisions and who depends on those decisions for a livelihood. This concept should not be read as a demand to take away every item people use personally; the focus is on social relations of production and power.

Movements and political ideas

Protest

Protest is political action intended to put pressure on authorities or institutions to change a policy or practice. It can shape public opinion, and it can be part of broader organising, strikes, negotiation or electoral action. Protest is not necessarily “only symbolic”; the effectiveness of a tactic depends on the demand, participation, balance of power and circumstances. In labour movements in Bangladesh, organising and follow-through matter alongside public protest.

Proudhonism

Proudhonism refers to ideas associated with the French thinker Pierre-Joseph Proudhon. His work raised questions about mutual cooperation, cooperatives and concentrated authority, and later became associated with anarchist traditions. Marx engaged in sharp theoretical debate with Proudhon, but anarchist thought contains many currents and should not be reduced to one label. Proudhon’s ideas can inform discussion of cooperatives or decentralised organisation in Bangladesh, though his nineteenth-century European proposals cannot simply be transferred to the present.
