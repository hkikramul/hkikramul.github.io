Burkina Faso’s Technology Academy: An Anticolonial Ambition Meets the Test of Development

How the ambition to build domestic engineering skills could shape development in a postcolonial state, and which questions remain unanswered

Political news and analysis · Facts checked: 24 November 2026

In brief: Burkina Faso inaugurated the Académie technologique du Faso (ATF) in Pabré on 23 November 2026. The government has announced a 60-hectare campus, a first-phase cost of 37.37 billion CFA francs, and an inaugural cohort of 250 students. The academy aims to train engineers in fields including metallurgy, mining, energy, aviation, nuclear technology, digital technology, and cybersecurity. It is an effort to build a foundation for technological capacity; opening an academy does not, by itself, guarantee technological self-reliance or development outcomes.

What happened, and why it matters

Burkina Faso, a landlocked country in West Africa, has inaugurated a new technology academy in Pabré. According to the government’s announcement, the academy is intended to train engineers locally in metallurgy, mechanical engineering, energy, nuclear technology, aviation, mining, digital technology, cybersecurity, and telecommunications. The African Press Agency reports that the first cohort will comprise 250 graduates from scientific and technical tracks. They will begin with a two-year preparatory phase before moving into specializations.

The project’s political language is “technological sovereignty”: reducing reliance on imported equipment used in health care, transport, agriculture, and defence by developing skills and innovation at home. This is more than a story about constructing an educational institution. It is part of a wider debate about how a state can gain greater control over knowledge, production, and strategic decisions.

Why dependence can persist after colonial rule

Political independence gives a state its own flag, government, and international recognition. But the structure of an economy does not change on independence day. In many places, colonial rule organized railways, ports, tax systems, and production to send raw materials outward while bringing higher-value manufactured goods and technologies in. If, after independence, a country continues to export raw materials and import machinery and expertise, while much of the design, repair, quality control, and profit from technology remain abroad, older relationships can persist in new commercial forms.

The concepts of “neocolonialism” and “dependency” are used to describe parts of this experience. In Neo-Colonialism: The Last Stage of Imperialism (1965), Kwame Nkrumah focused on unequal economic and financial control that can continue after political independence. Frantz Fanon’s The Wretched of the Earth (1961) warned that if national elites do not transform production and social power after independence, its benefits may remain limited in ordinary people’s lives. These are analytical frameworks, not one explanation that applies identically to every country.

Anticolonialism, then, is not simply opposition to a former colonial power. A practical measure is whether a country can choose technologies in the public interest, learn and adapt them, make or repair essential goods, and retain more value from its natural resources. Foreign cooperation is not inherently a problem; the central concern is lasting dependence on unequal terms without building local capacity.

What development paths could the academy open?

Technical education can change an economy when it connects to real production and social needs. Knowledge in mining and metallurgy could support processing minerals at home instead of exporting them raw. Energy and electrical engineering could contribute to the design and maintenance of power systems and the use of renewable sources. Mechanical engineering, chemistry, and biotechnology could support local production in food processing, medicines, and medical equipment. Digital skills could build domestic capacity in public services, communications, and cybersecurity. These are potential pathways, not outcomes already achieved.

If the state pays for buildings but does not provide long-term faculty appointments, research funding, equipment maintenance, links to industry, fair hiring, and jobs for graduates, the academy could remain largely symbolic. Specialized laboratories and equipment are expensive; officials have said that these will be installed gradually. A strong curriculum, experienced teachers, safe working conditions, and clear routes into further study and industry are therefore as important as the opening ceremony.

Sovereignty also requires accountability

Technological self-reliance does not mean making everything alone. No country can independently produce every chip, aircraft, medicine, or research instrument. The goal should be to understand needed technologies, adapt, repair, and improve them locally, work with multiple countries on fair terms, and create alternatives for critical supplies. Regional cooperation and knowledge exchange can also strengthen sovereignty.

The public should also be able to ask: How will the 37.37 billion CFA-franc expenditure be audited? What standards will govern admissions and faculty recruitment? How many students will graduate, and where will they work? How transparent will research results and public contracts be? Will the academy’s technologies address problems faced by farmers, patients, workers, and small businesses, or will benefits concentrate in a small number of state or commercial institutions? These are not hostile questions. They are ordinary accountability questions for an institution built with public money.

Lessons for Bangladesh

The question of education and technology is familiar in Bangladesh as well. Across garments, pharmaceuticals, agriculture, shipbuilding, electricity, and information technology, it is important not only to expand production but also to build design, equipment maintenance, materials, quality control, research, and high-skill work at home. A slogan about national ownership is not enough: universities, technical education, industrial policy, workers’ skills and rights, public procurement, and research investment must fit together.

Burkina Faso’s academy is not a model Bangladesh can copy exactly; the two countries differ in their economies, institutions, populations, and political realities. But it leaves a useful question: will we only buy technologies from abroad, or will we also build the ability to understand, adapt, repair, and create knowledge for our own social needs?

What to watch in the years ahead

Several indicators will matter: the quality and diversity of students and faculty; whether laboratories are used effectively; the volume of research and innovation; partnerships with local industry; graduate employment and opportunities to work at home; the quality and cost of locally made or repaired products; access for women and rural students; and independent audits of project costs and outcomes. It will also be important to assess whether dependence on imported technology is actually declining and how much of any resulting benefit reaches the wider public.

The hardest test of anticolonial politics is therefore found in institutions, not speeches: who produces knowledge, who captures the value of production, and how much say the public has over development decisions. Burkina Faso’s new academy is an ambitious beginning to that test. Its success will be shaped by the education, research, and public accountability that follow.
