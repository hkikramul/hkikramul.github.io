Medical Education and Patient Safety in Bangladesh: Financial, Psychological and Health-System Consequences

Evidence-informed analysis · East Bengal Renaissance · 1 November 2026

বাংলা সংস্করণ পড়ুন →

Abstract

Formal qualifications are important indicators of professional preparation, but they cannot independently guarantee safe care. Competency-based training, effective supervision, diagnostic capacity, continuity of information and institutional accountability jointly shape patient outcomes. This narrative review considers Bangladesh’s medical education framework alongside comparative educational research, clinical guidelines and health-economic evidence. The available evidence does not establish entry to medical school after higher secondary education as an independent cause of patient harm. A more defensible reform agenda centres on demonstrated competence, transparent care, and financial and psychosocial protection for patients and families.

Context and scope

A second-hand account from an acquaintance’s family described costly surgery for a suspected brain infection, subsequent referral, later information about a herpesvirus diagnosis, and dissatisfaction over access to testing-related materials. This account motivated the discussion; it is neither research data nor a verified case of medical negligence. Clinical records, the treating teams’ accounts and an independent specialist assessment were unavailable. No conclusion is therefore drawn about the appropriateness of an individual decision or the liability of an institution. Identifying details have been omitted.

This article is a narrative review of selected research and policy sources, not a systematic review. The cited studies differ in country, period, sampling and outcome measurement. International estimates are not presented as Bangladesh-specific rates, and statistical associations are not treated as proof of causation.

Entry pathways versus demonstrated competence

One argument in debates about medical education in Bangladesh is that entering medical school directly after higher secondary education may leave doctors insufficiently prepared. Establishing this claim would require distinguishing the effects of entry pathway, selection, curriculum and training environment, and relating them to patient outcomes. Comparing the structures of two countries’ educational systems cannot, by itself, identify a causal mechanism.

Under the Bangladesh Medical & Dental Council’s 2021 curriculum, the MBBS programme lasts five years. A further year of supervised internship is required for permanent registration for independent practice. Higher secondary completion is therefore the entry point to a substantial professional training programme, not an immediate licence to practise. Graduate entry to medical school—following an earlier university degree—must also be distinguished from postgraduate specialist training after the MBBS. [1]

Reid and colleagues compared clinical assessment results for 713 students across four cohorts at an Australian medical school. Graduate-entry and undergraduate-entry students undertaking the same clinical curriculum performed similarly. This finding does not support a categorical claim of inferior competence based on entry pathway. However, it concerns student assessments at one institution, not long-term patient safety or the quality of Bangladeshi medical schools. [2]

The distinction matters for policy. A proposal to require a prior degree should be assessed against potential educational benefits, additional time and expense, and implications for access among students from less affluent backgrounds. Before changing entry requirements, measuring what graduates can actually do provides a more direct basis for reform.

Postgraduate training and institutional assurance of competence

A recognised specialist qualification is a meaningful professional achievement. Nevertheless, assurance of competence in a particular clinical area also requires attention to training content, supervised experience, assessment and application of current knowledge. It is unjustified to label an individual’s credentials “low quality” without evidence; equally, the existence of a qualification should not foreclose questions about care quality.

Cook and colleagues’ systematic review and meta-analysis of 82 studies found that simulation-based mastery learning—requiring learners to meet a defined standard before progressing—was associated with improved skills and some patient outcomes compared with no intervention. Evidence against other active instructional approaches was more limited, and mastery learning required additional time. These results do not evaluate all training institutions in Bangladesh. They support assessing attained competence alongside training duration and certification. [3]

Quality assurance should therefore examine direct observation in clinical work, constructive feedback, progressive authorisation of responsibilities and access to experienced supervision for difficult decisions. Staffing, laboratory capacity and information systems must also be considered. Attributing every problem to an individual doctor can obscure organisational failures.

Diagnosis, surgery and communication: the encephalitis example

Herpes simplex virus can cause encephalitis, or inflammation of the brain. The 2026 EAN–ESCMID guideline recommends starting intravenous aciclovir as soon as possible when infectious encephalitis is suspected and highlights appropriate pathogen-specific cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) PCR testing. These are professional management recommendations, not instructions for readers to start or alter medication themselves. [4]

Routine brain surgery is not the standard method for diagnosing HSV encephalitis; the IDSA guideline does not recommend routine brain biopsy. This general point cannot establish that a particular operation was unnecessary. Contemporaneous clinical records are needed to determine whether another diagnosis, tissue sampling or management of a complication motivated the procedure. [5]

Technical success of an operation, control of the underlying illness and overall recovery are distinct outcomes. Referral is not inherently evidence of failure. The relevant questions concern the rationale and timing of decisions and the transfer of necessary clinical information. Biological specimens, tissue blocks, slides and written laboratory reports are also distinct materials. Whether an original specimen can be handed directly to relatives may depend on applicable policy and safety requirements. Patients nevertheless need clear explanations of what was tested, the result and how relevant records can be obtained.

Making records available to patients or authorised representatives is an important ethical principle reflected in the American Medical Association’s guidance. That guidance is cited as an ethical reference, not as a statement of Bangladeshi law. [6]

Financial consequences for patients and households

Economic consequences should be separated into direct expenditure and indirect losses. Additional investigations, treatment, hospital stays and rehabilitation constitute direct costs. Lost patient earnings, caregivers’ time away from work and redistribution of household responsibilities represent indirect burdens. Debt, depleted savings and asset sales may extend the impact beyond the episode of illness. However, expenditure on necessary care is not equivalent to additional expenditure attributable to deficient care. Assessing losses in an individual case requires distinguishing the two.

Using data from 14,395 households in Bangladesh’s Household Income and Expenditure Survey 2022, Sarker and colleagues estimated catastrophic health expenditure in approximately 17.72% of households at a threshold of 10% of total household consumption. At a threshold of 40% of non-food expenditure, the estimate was 9.46%. Their analysis also estimated that out-of-pocket health spending pushed approximately 3.7% of the population below the poverty line. These estimates depend on the selected thresholds, poverty definition and analytical method. [7]

These findings describe financial vulnerability associated with health spending generally; they do not measure losses caused by medical error. Household recall and reporting affect the data, and the indicators do not fully capture people who needed care but could not afford to seek it. Financial protection must therefore consider both those paying for services and those excluded from them.

Psychological consequences and trust

Unexpected harm or unresolved uncertainty about treatment can generate fear, anger, guilt and helplessness among patients and relatives. Separating the psychological effects of illness from those of the care experience is not always straightforward. Rather than assigning a psychiatric diagnosis to every affected family, services should assess individual experiences and support needs.

A Massachusetts survey of 253 respondents reporting an experience of medical error found that, within the subgroup whose experience occurred three to six years earlier, 51% reported at least one persisting emotional impact and 67% reported loss of trust. More open communication was associated with lower levels of some adverse emotional outcomes. The observational, self-reported design cannot establish causation, and the estimates are not prevalence figures for Bangladesh. [8]

The policy implication is that explanations and access to information are more than administrative courtesies. They relate to patients’ experiences, subsequent healthcare engagement and trust. Responses to complaints, psychosocial support and continuing communication should be considered components of care quality.

Public expenditure and opportunity costs

Hospital beds, intensive care, medicines and staff time used to manage preventable complications could otherwise serve other patients. This opportunity cost matters especially where capacity is constrained. Long-term disability, reduced employment among patients and caregivers, and increased needs for social support can impose further societal burdens. Necessary intensive care or appropriate referral should not, however, be classified as waste on this basis.

The OECD estimates that approximately 15% of hospital activity and expenditure in its member countries is attributable to patient harm. This is not an estimate for Bangladesh, and patient harm is not synonymous with negligence. [9] Separately, Kruk and colleagues used 2016 data to model approximately five million deaths associated with poor-quality care across 137 low- and middle-income countries. This finding demonstrates the importance of health-system quality; it is neither a national count of medical negligence in Bangladesh nor an estimate of the effect of a particular medical-school entry pathway. [10]

Policy implications

The evidence supports considering reform in three connected areas. First, medical education requires objective assessment of competence and adequate capacity for supervision. Second, explanations of diagnosis, alternatives, risks, expected costs and uncertainty should accompany authorised access to records and reliable transfer of information. Third, review mechanisms should be independent of conflicts of interest, provide fair hearings for both patients and clinicians, and enable appropriate remedies where harm is established.

Raw comparisons of mortality or readmission rates are insufficient for evaluating institutions. Without considering initial severity and case mix, hospitals accepting the most complex patients may appear unjustifiably poor. Financial protection policies likewise need to distinguish the cost of necessary treatment, avoidable harm and unmet need.

Reform of medical education and protection of patients’ rights are compatible goals. Effective institutions allow clinicians to work with adequate support, give patients respectful explanations, and turn identified errors into opportunities for correction rather than concealment. The ultimate test of education and governance is the capacity to provide safe, effective and financially accessible care to real patients.
