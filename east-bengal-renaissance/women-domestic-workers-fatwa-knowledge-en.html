Religion and society · Knowledge and authority · Dignity of labour

“A domestic worker issues a fatwa”: the Qur’an, Islamic history, and the right to knowledge

Abstract: A social-media image says, “Even domestic maids nowadays issue fatwas against Mufti Amir Hamza”—and calls this laughable. The image does not provide the alleged fatwa, its source, context, or supporting evidence. This essay therefore does not decide whether any particular fatwa is sound, nor does it assess any specific statement by Amir Hamza. It examines the visible argument in the image: using a woman’s occupation and class position to discredit her speech. It considers that argument through four lenses: Qur’anic ethics of justice, the meaning and qualifications of fatwa, women’s historical participation in Islamic learning, and the dignity of labour.

In this essay: What the image does and does not establish · Fatwa and qualification · Women in Islamic learning · Qur’anic ethics and labour · A fair method of criticism · References

1. What the image says—and what it does not

The claim has two parts. First, someone supposedly issued a “fatwa” against Mufti Amir Hamza. Second, the act is presented as laughable because the speaker is described as a “domestic maid.” But the image gives no text of the fatwa, no identity or qualifications of its author, no question that prompted it, no cited evidence, and no indication whether it was a formal fatwa or ordinary criticism. Those missing details cannot responsibly be treated as established facts.

This limit matters. Evaluating a religious opinion requires its original wording and context. The only material available here is the sentence in the image. This essay is not a verdict for or against Amir Hamza; it examines the use of social rank as a substitute for argument.

2. What is a fatwa, and where does qualification matter?

A fatwa is an answer or opinion on a question of Islamic law or religious practice, grounded in knowledge and method. It is generally distinct from a court judgment or state law, although its social and institutional authority varies across communities and settings. Egypt’s Dar al-Ifta describes fatwa as the work of clarifying Islamic legal rulings and stresses that the mufti should understand the time, place, people, and circumstances of a question. The Fiqh Council of North America likewise describes a fatwa as an evidence-based legal opinion issued by a qualified person, generally non-binding in itself. [1] [2]

Two separate conclusions follow. First, giving responsible religious guidance requires relevant learning, familiarity with sources and interpretive methods, and attention to context. Qur’an 16:43—“ask the people of knowledge if you do not know”—appears in a specific discussion about asking people knowledgeable in earlier scriptures about the humanity of previous messengers. It should not be detached from that context and treated as a complete rule for every dispute about fatwas. It is nevertheless related to the broader practice of consulting people with relevant knowledge. [3]

Second, a person’s social class cannot determine whether an argument is true. It is legitimate to ask whether someone is qualified to issue a formal fatwa, but the questions should be relevant: What training or expertise does the person have? Which sources are cited? Does the reasoning follow a recognized method? How does it address competing interpretations? “She is a domestic worker, so her view is laughable” answers none of these questions. It replaces engagement with the argument by a judgment about the speaker’s social position.

Conversely, calling someone a “mufti” does not make every statement infallible. A title, public following, or political identity cannot replace examination of evidence and reasoning. Islamic legal traditions contain interpretive disagreement and context-sensitive judgments; opinions should be assessed at that level.

3. Women’s place in the history of Islamic learning

The claim that women cannot acquire or transmit religious knowledge is difficult to reconcile with the Islamic record. In Sahih al-Bukhari 101, Abu Sa‘id al-Khudri reports that women asked the Prophet Muhammad to set aside a day for their instruction; he agreed and taught them. The report documents women’s demand for learning and their participation as students. [4]

‘A’isha’s role is especially significant. She transmitted hadith, taught, and served as a source of knowledge in the early Muslim community. Later Islamic biographical and scholarly traditions remember her as a source of guidance on religious and legal questions. Egypt’s Dar al-Ifta’s historical overview discusses women scholars and muftis, including ‘A’isha, and points to women’s learning recorded in medieval biographical works. The institution has its own theological standpoint, so its overview should be read alongside primary sources and broader scholarship rather than treated as the sole historical authority. [5]

This history does not prove that every woman may issue a fatwa on every subject. It supports a narrower and more defensible conclusion: gender alone is not proof of intellectual incapacity. Whether a woman or a man claims authority in Islamic law, the relevant question is whether their knowledge, method, and evidence support the claim. Standards of qualification may exist; they should not be confused with ridicule based on a person’s occupation or class.

4. Qur’anic ethics, social rank, and the dignity of labour

Qur’an 49:11 prohibits one group from ridiculing another and explicitly warns women against ridiculing other women. Verse 49:13 relates human distinction not to social status or lineage but to moral conduct and God-consciousness. Qur’an 4:135 calls for justice even when testimony goes against oneself or one’s relatives. Read together, these verses challenge the practice of dismissing someone’s speech through contempt for their identity. [6] [7]

The phrase “domestic maid” is often used as a demeaning class label for women who perform domestic work. Domestic workers provide necessary labour: cleaning, cooking, and caring for children or older people, among other tasks. Their occupation does not reduce their human dignity or their right to participate in religious and public discussion. Dismissing their voices in advance is especially consequential because it targets workers with less social power and fewer opportunities to make themselves heard.

Sahih al-Bukhari 2545 reports that the Prophet Muhammad rebuked Abu Dharr for insulting a man through his mother’s identity. In the historical context of slavery, the report calls dependent people “brothers” and addresses their food, clothing, and workload. Historical slavery and contemporary paid domestic work are not the same institution and should not be collapsed into one. The report’s ethical rejection of insult and social arrogance is nonetheless relevant to the language under discussion. [8]

5. What does fair criticism look like?

When a religious statement or fatwa is disputed, a responsible process can follow several steps: (a) locate the original statement or document; (b) check the full context; (c) distinguish a formal fatwa from a personal opinion or political criticism; (d) examine the Qur’anic, hadith, juristic, and factual evidence used; (e) represent competing interpretations fairly; and (f) allow the speaker an opportunity to respond.

This standard applies equally to Amir Hamza and to his critics. Calling him a scholar does not place his statements beyond question. Criticising him does not become laughable because the critic is a woman or domestic worker. The proper subject of criticism is the claim, evidence, and method—not the speaker’s class position.

Conclusion

The image does not provide enough evidence to assess any particular fatwa. It does, however, make a clear class-based suggestion: a domestic worker’s contribution to religious debate is laughable. That suggestion is difficult to defend in light of the Qur’an’s prohibition of ridicule, its demand for justice, the historical participation of women in Islamic learning, and the dignity owed to workers. At the same time, the qualifications behind a fatwa and the soundness of any particular opinion should be examined with evidence. Responsible evaluation means judging arguments through knowledge and proof, not declaring people unqualified through contempt for their social status.
