“She doesn’t work”? The economic value of unpaid household labour in Bangladesh

Cooking, cleaning, washing clothes and caring for children or ill relatives produce essential services even when no wage is paid. Having no personal earnings is therefore different from producing nothing of economic value. Research and official time-use statistics from Bangladesh help make that distinction visible.

This discussion begins with homemakers, but the national estimates below are not restricted to housewives. They may include unpaid work by employed women, students and others. They do not establish a particular homemaker’s monthly wage or entitlement.

1. How much time does the work take?

The Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics’ Time Use Survey 2021 reports that women aged 15 and above spent an average of 5.9 hours daily on unpaid domestic and care work, compared with 0.8 hours for men. These are population averages for the respective groups, not averages restricted to participants in those activities. The reported total is used here; separately rounded components may not add exactly. [1]

This difference illustrates how counting only paid employment can obscure labour. However, the survey took place during the pandemic, so its results should not be treated as an unchanging description of every year.

The first figure covers women and men; the second headline figure covers women alone. They do not have identical scope. Nor does comparing the older CPD estimate with newer figures establish a decline in the value of unpaid work. Reference periods, activity coverage, wage assumptions, samples and accounting choices must be reconciled before comparison.

3. How can work without wages be valued?

Replacement cost asks what it would cost to purchase an equivalent service. A basic framework multiplies working hours by a relevant hourly wage. Sen and colleagues use an input-based replacement-cost approach with generalist wage rates. [3]

Using one wage for all tasks or separate wages for cooking, childcare and other services can produce different estimates. Opportunity cost asks what the person could have earned in alternative work; it is not the same as replacement cost. Willingness to accept values work through the compensation considered acceptable for undertaking it; CPD used it as a separate approach. [4]

For illustration only, six hours daily valued at an assumed BDT 100 per hour would equal 6 × 100 × 30 = BDT 18,000 over 30 days. This is not a verified Bangladeshi market wage or a national estimate of a homemaker’s contribution.

4. Outside GDP does not mean without value

Unpaid domestic and care services produced for one’s own household generally fall outside the core national-accounts production boundary. This does not mean all unpaid production is excluded: some goods produced for own use are treated differently. A household-production satellite account provides a broader view alongside the core accounts. [2]

“Equivalent to 18.9% of GDP” does not mean that amount has already been added to published GDP, that new cash income has appeared, or that the state pays women a corresponding amount. Nor can the same percentage simply be described as the share of a newly expanded GDP: the denominator would change.

5. What should recognition achieve?

Recognising this labour does not establish that women should remain at home. Meaningful choices about paid work, education, rest and household roles matter. A person’s dignity also does not depend on the market value assigned to their labour.

Policy proposals informed by this discussion include sharing household responsibilities, affordable quality childcare, support for older people and persons with disabilities, time-saving water and energy infrastructure, and social protection for caregivers. These are proposals here, not claims that every intervention has been proven effective in Bangladesh. Any proposed homemaker allowance or wage also requires assessment of eligibility, financing, recipients’ control over money and possible effects on women’s freedom to pursue other roles.

6. Conclusion

Specific research on the economic value of unpaid household and care work exists in Bangladesh. Homemakers produce necessary services even without personal earnings. National valuation can make that contribution visible; dignity, rest and a voice in decisions do not require passing a monetary valuation test.
