A Sustainable Way to Reduce Inequality: How Can Wages Rise Without Overloading the Economy?

East Bengal Renaissance · November 23, 2026

In one part of the economy, workers’ pay can remain stuck for years; elsewhere, wealth can grow rapidly. This raises a difficult question: can wages rise fairly without damaging businesses and jobs? And if wages do not rise, how are working people supposed to cover rent, food, health care, and transport?

A sustainable solution does not promise to fix everything in one stroke. It builds rules that allow wages to rise regularly, give workers a voice in bargaining, give small businesses time to adjust, and let government revise policy in light of results.

What the image’s $20 proposal tells us—and what it does not

The image says the U.S. federal minimum wage was $7.25 an hour in both 2009 and 2026. The U.S. Department of Labor confirms that the federal rate has been $7.25 since July 24, 2009. The image also compares the combined wealth of the 400 richest Americans: $1.27 trillion in 2009 and $8 trillion in 2026. These are amounts in current dollars; they are not adjusted for inflation.

The comparison makes a widening wealth gap visible, but those two figures alone do not prove that exactly $20 an hour is the right minimum wage. The $20 figure is a U.S. policy demand, not a formula that can be copied directly in Bangladesh or elsewhere. Wage setting must account for local living costs, productivity, employment, and what businesses can sustain.

Seven steps toward a practical wage system

1. Set a national wage floor and raise it gradually

There should be a clear national minimum so that every worker has a basic legal wage protection. A published schedule that raises the rate over several years lets both workers and employers plan ahead. The pace should take account of inflation, median wages, productivity, poverty, and employment data.

2. Stop purchasing power from eroding again

Once the target is reached, the minimum could be updated automatically each year. Indexing to inflation protects what workers can buy; linking it to median wages allows workers at the bottom to share in broader wage growth. The law should state the formula clearly so that adjustments do not repeatedly get stuck in political bargaining.

3. Make the national wage a floor, not a ceiling

Housing and transport costs differ across cities and regions. The national rate should provide basic protection, while local governments or sectoral bodies may set higher rates where costs are greater. Where local data are weak, estimates should be treated as provisional and checked through regular, transparent cost surveys.

4. Give workers a voice and enforce the law

If wages are set only through decisions by employers and government, workers’ experience can be left out. Unions, collective bargaining, and sectoral wage boards should include workers, employers, and independent experts. Effective inspections and safe complaint channels are needed to address wage theft, misclassification, and illegal deductions. For tipped work, workers should receive the applicable minimum wage from employers rather than having tips substitute for it; this makes pay more predictable.

5. Offer temporary, targeted support to small businesses

The cost of adjustment may be difficult for small, low-margin firms. Government could provide time-limited tax credits, technology assistance, or accessible loans after checking a firm’s size and need. Support should not become a permanent subsidy or flow to large, highly profitable firms. Policymakers should also examine avoidable intermediary costs, excessive staff turnover, and barriers to productivity.

6. Pair wages with public services and income support

Higher hourly pay alone cannot solve problems with rent, health care, child care, or transport. Good public health care, affordable child care, public transit, education, and refundable tax credits for low-income families can raise people’s effective income. That means employers are not asked to carry the entire burden of improving living standards.

7. Measure results and adjust when needed

After each phase, publish data on real wages, poverty, hours, hiring, prices, business closures, and workers’ living conditions. If an unintended problem appears in a particular region or sector, the schedule or support can be adjusted. Evaluation should rely on evidence, not one employer’s experience or a political slogan.

There are benefits and risks

Higher wages can raise the earnings of many low-paid workers and move some families above the poverty line. At the same time, costs rise for some firms; depending on circumstances, they may increase prices, cut hours, or delay hiring. The Congressional Budget Office’s 2023 analysis examined a proposed phased increase in the U.S. federal rate to $17 an hour by 2029, followed by adjustments tied to median hourly wages. Its analysis showed that many workers could gain income while some workers faced a risk of losing jobs. It was not a direct forecast of the effects of a $20 minimum wage.

So the question is not simply “Should wages rise?” It is how quickly they should rise, which evidence should guide the rate, who should take part in decisions, how workers and businesses should be supported through the transition, and how policymakers will respond if results are poor.

Equality does not mean everyone earns the same

From a social democratic perspective, the goal is a fair economic floor: people should not remain in poverty despite working full time, and the costs of economic change should not be pushed solely onto small businesses or workers. The task is to balance workers’ fair share, businesses’ ability to produce sustainably, and public services that people need.

For Bangladesh, wage policy should be built around verified local living costs, workers’ experience, industries’ ability to pay, and regular review. The transferable lesson is not the U.S. $20 figure; it is transparent calculation, gradual implementation, and shared participation.
