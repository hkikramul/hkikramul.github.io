Policy research draft

A Social Democratic AI Policy for Bangladesh

9 November 2026

Download the English thesis (Word) · AI Policy Tracker

Abstract

Artificial intelligence can raise output, improve access to services, and help workers perform unfamiliar tasks. Its deployment can also change hiring, pay, surveillance, and access to essential services. These effects depend on what a system does and on who has the power to determine its use. This thesis asks how a social democratic government in Bangladesh could use AI for public and economic benefit while giving people enforceable rights over consequential decisions. It synthesizes research on productivity, labor demand, algorithmic management, fairness, and auditing, and compares social democratic party platforms with enacted laws and expert guidance. It proposes a risk-based law, a deployment protocol for high-risk uses, worker information and bargaining rights, public procurement rules, and investment in accessible Bangla-language AI. Two hypothetical cases illustrate the framework. An empirical research design specifies how future pilots could assess effects on access, employment, earnings, and working conditions. The thesis is a desk-based policy analysis and proposes measures for testing. It contains no original field data or estimates of AI-related job loss in Bangladesh.

Keywords — AI regulation, algorithmic management, labor markets, social democracy, Bangladesh

1 Introduction

A firm can use AI to help a worker complete a task, to replace part of that task, or to monitor how the worker performs it. The same firm might adopt all three uses at once. A public agency can use AI to help staff find information or to prioritize applications for assistance. Each deployment creates a different question about what evidence is sufficient, who should have a say, and what a person can do when the system causes harm.

The thesis argues that a governing agenda for Bangladesh should combine public investment with enforceable rights. The state can help make technology useful through public procurement, training, language resources, and shared research capacity. Law can define duties for high-risk deployments. Collective bargaining can determine how workplace changes affect pay, workload, hours, and job security. An appeals process can correct individual decisions. These functions require different authorities and procedures.

The national policy process offers a current point of entry. As of 9 November 2026, the official Bangladesh AI policy portal reports that the ICT Division constituted a formulation committee on 2 November and asked it to submit a draft by 15 December. This is the committee’s submission deadline. The portal says it will announce further steps separately (ICT Division, 2026).

1.1 Research questions

  • What does the literature establish about AI’s effects on task productivity, labor demand, working conditions, and the distribution of gains?
  • Which safeguards can Bangladesh adapt from international laws, social democratic proposals, and expert frameworks?
  • How should Bangladesh assign responsibility for evaluating high-risk systems, negotiating workplace changes, and remedying harms?
  • What evidence would show whether the proposed policy works in practice?

1.2 Scope and approach

The study uses a structured narrative review and comparative policy analysis. It draws on selected peer-reviewed economics and computing research, working papers, official legal and regulatory texts, party manifestos, and institutional reports. The search focused on task exposure, workplace adoption, labor-market outcomes, worker voice, algorithmic fairness, and mechanisms for pre- and post-deployment oversight. Foundational research was included where it clarified a mechanism. Bangladesh sources were used to establish the policy and institutional context. The review is thematic and has no exhaustive database protocol or pooled effect estimate.

The comparison assesses each instrument by legal status, covered decision, duty holder, review mechanism, worker participation, and remedy. Case selection is purposive. Germany, Spain, the EU, the United Kingdom’s labor movement, Australia, and Canada illustrate different policy instruments. They do not constitute a representative sample of countries or parties. No inference about the effect of an entire national regime follows from this comparison.

1.3 Key distinctions

An AI system is high risk under the proposed framework when its intended use materially influences a consequential decision or creates a substantial risk to rights or livelihoods. A generic tool can become high risk through its use. The deployment protocol is the written, auditable plan for that use. Regulation establishes when the plan is required and what remedies apply. Automated workplace management should be covered where it has the same material effects, including rules-based systems that a supplier does not market as AI.

2 Literature Review

2.1 Automation operates through tasks

Acemoglu and Restrepo (2019) distinguish between the displacement of labor in tasks taken over by capital and the creation of new tasks that generate demand for labor. Productivity can raise demand and expand production, but the distribution of the resulting income depends on the mix of displacement, new tasks, and market conditions. This framework explains why automating a task can reduce employment in one job category while expanding output or employment elsewhere.

Applied to generative AI, it suggests measuring changes at several levels. A translator may spend less time producing a first draft and more time checking terminology. A firm may respond by handling more orders, by hiring fewer junior staff, or by reducing prices. A platform may alter its fee or payment rules. An occupational exposure score captures only part of this chain. Analysis also needs the rate of adoption, the quality of the substitute, demand for the service, and the ability of workers to negotiate.

2.2 Productivity evidence varies by task and setting

Noy and Zhang (2023) used an experiment involving professional writing tasks to test the effects of generative AI assistance. Participants assigned access completed tasks more quickly and achieved higher evaluated quality on average. The design supports causal inference about the studied tasks. It does not establish effects on wages, employment, or the performance of teams after an organization changes its workflow.

Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond (2025) studied an AI assistant in customer support. The published study estimates an average productivity gain of roughly 15 percent, with larger gains among less experienced and lower-skilled workers. It suggests that a tool can help disseminate effective work practices. Effects depend on the organization’s workflow, available training data, and how performance is measured. A firm-level estimate cannot be transported directly to Bangladesh’s outsourcing sector.

Humlum and Vestergaard (2025) link adoption survey data to administrative labor records in Denmark. Their revised working paper finds task changes and occupational switching but little measurable short-run change in earnings or recorded hours in the studied settings. This result can coexist with substantial productivity gains on particular tasks. Firms may adjust slowly, saved time may be used for new work, and contracts or bargaining arrangements can mediate changes in pay and hours. The paper supplies an early labor-market comparison and has limited external validity for informal work in Bangladesh.

2.3 Exposure and realized labor-market effects

The ILO’s 2025 global index estimates that around one in four workers is employed in an occupation with some exposure to generative AI. Its task-based analysis suggests that changes to jobs are more likely than complete replacement across many occupations. The World Bank’s South Asia analysis estimates that around 7 percent of jobs are highly exposed with limited potential for AI complementarity and that around 15 percent could benefit substantially from complementarity. Neither estimate measures job losses. The models use different concepts and units and should not be combined to produce a Bangladesh forecast (ILO, 2025; World Bank, 2025).

The main evidence gap concerns the transmission from exposure to outcomes. For outsourcing, foreign clients can change prices or bring work in-house even if local employers have not adopted AI. For entry-level work, fewer routine assignments may change how new workers build expertise. For manufacturing, generative AI used in office tasks has a different effect from machine vision used in inspection. In app-based work, automated pricing or suspension can reduce earnings while leaving the number of registered workers unchanged. National monitoring therefore needs to measure entry, exit, wages, hours, and work intensity alongside adoption.

2.4 Algorithmic management and collective bargaining

Kellogg, Valentine, and Christin (2020) synthesize research on how algorithms direct, evaluate, and discipline labor. The issue extends beyond whether a prediction is accurate. Software can change who sees performance data, how targets are set, and what forms of resistance or correction are available. The OECD’s employer survey on algorithmic management identifies reported benefits alongside concerns about accountability, interpretability, and worker health. Employer reports document perceived effects and have limited ability to establish worker outcomes or causal effects (Milanez et al., 2025).

The OECD’s employment outlook treats social dialogue and collective bargaining as mechanisms for shaping AI use (OECD, 2023). Their effectiveness depends on whether workers can organize, access information, and negotiate without retaliation. Notice allows people to learn that a system exists. Consultation gives representatives an opportunity to influence its design. Bargaining gives them a route to negotiate changes to employment conditions. Co-determination can go further by requiring agreement for covered decisions. A statute should specify which of these powers applies, to whom, and at what stage.

2.5 Fairness, documentation, and accountability

Selbst et al. (2019) warn against evaluating fairness by abstracting away the social context of a decision. An apparently accurate hiring model may learn from past managerial ratings that incorporate unequal access to training or advancement. Changing a statistical metric cannot resolve a dispute about which criteria count as valid evidence of ability. Raghavan et al. (2020) document how choices about training data, prediction targets, and validation shape claims made by hiring-tool vendors. Their analysis supports requiring job-relevant validation at the intended deployment site.

Raji et al. (2020) propose an internal audit covering the AI lifecycle, with documentation at each stage. Internal auditing helps preserve a record of design choices, data limitations, and unresolved risks. Independent scrutiny serves a different purpose when organizational incentives favor deployment. For high-risk uses, an external reviewer should be able to inspect the evidence confidentially. Affected people also need a remedy that remains available after an audit ends.

2.6 Synthesis and research gap

Research on task productivity provides a basis for testing specific benefits, while labor-market studies show why aggregate outcomes require direct measurement. The algorithmic management literature adds the distribution of authority inside the workplace. Fairness and audit research shows how technical evidence can be examined and preserved. International guidance supplies tools for risk assessment, but the political and institutional question is who can require changes, suspend a system, or obtain a remedy. This thesis connects those strands through a Bangladesh-specific proposal. Whether the combined approach improves outcomes remains an empirical question.

2.7 Development, market power, and public capacity

Korinek and Stiglitz (2021) examine how AI and automation could affect developing countries through trade, specialization, and the distribution of returns to capital. A country may face changing demand for labor-intensive exports even when local AI adoption is limited. Domestic productivity policies therefore need to be paired with attention to where profits, capabilities, and control accumulate. These economic mechanisms do not establish the size of any effect in Bangladesh.

UNCTAD (2025) examines the uneven distribution of computing, data, skills, and research capacity. Simply purchasing access to models can leave public agencies and smaller firms dependent on suppliers. Shared evaluation resources can reduce duplicated costs, but their value depends on quality, licensing, and who can use them. Procurement can reduce dependence through access to records, interoperable interfaces, and exit terms.

2.8 Public administration and platform work

Eubanks (2018) documents cases of automated administration in the United States and shows how errors and administrative burdens can fall on people least able to contest them. These cases offer mechanisms to investigate in Bangladesh. Public pilots should assess the entire service process, including applicants who abandon it, appeals that never reach a reviewer, and burdens shifted from the agency to households.

The ILO (2021) examines how platforms organize work across different markets. A remote freelancer faces changes in international demand and contracting. A delivery rider may depend on one platform for task allocation and earnings. A common right to reasons and review can apply to consequential decisions while bargaining arrangements reflect the different markets.

3 International Policy Comparison

3.1 Social democratic and labor movement proposals

Germany’s SPD proposed in its 2025 manifesto to strengthen works-council co-determination for introducing AI and other workplace changes, including a requirement to reach agreement for covered matters. The Party of European Socialists’ 2024 manifesto called for human control and protection of worker rights. In October 2026, the European Parliament’s Socialists and Democrats group advocated legislation on algorithmic management and workplace AI within its Quality Jobs Act agenda. These sources establish political commitments and advocacy (SPD, 2025; PES, 2024; S&D, 2026).

Australia’s Labor government consulted on mandatory high-risk AI safeguards. The government’s consultation page says it is setting the earlier proposal aside for the time being. Its National AI Plan emphasizes investment, adoption, and worker training. The Australian example illustrates a governing choice about how quickly to expand adoption and when to impose dedicated regulation. A Bangladesh strategy can combine investment and training with a separate legislative process for enforceable rights (Australian Department of Industry, Science and Resources, 2024–2025).

The UK Trades Union Congress proposed a model bill in 2024 that would require union consultation for certain high-risk and intrusive uses and provide human reconsideration of high-risk decisions. Its relevance lies in the duties it would put into legislation. It is a labor movement proposal (TUC, 2024).

3.2 Enacted instruments

Spain’s 2021 Riders’ Law added a right for worker representatives to obtain information on the parameters, rules, and instructions behind algorithms affecting working conditions or access to and retention of employment. It also created a separate presumption applicable to delivery work under specified conditions. The information right helps representatives identify how management rules operate. Employment classification and the content of an information right address distinct issues (Spain, 2021).

Articles 10 and 11 of the EU Platform Work Directive address oversight and human review. The directive requires Member States to ensure that platforms evaluate the effects of automated monitoring and decision-making at least every two years with worker representatives involved. It gives a sector-specific precedent for periodic review after deployment. The EU AI Act provides a wider framework for risk classification, risk management, and human oversight, including specified employment uses. The instruments differ in scope and duties (European Union, 2024a, 2024b).

3.3 Expert and administrative frameworks

UNESCO’s Recommendation on the Ethics of AI provides a basis for impact assessment and participatory oversight. NIST’s voluntary AI Risk Management Framework organizes activities around governance, mapping, measurement, and management. Canada’s federal administrative approach uses an algorithmic impact assessment to scale requirements and calls for peer review for systems above a defined impact threshold. Their legal status and scope differ. Bangladesh could draw from them to design a standard assessment, decide which uses need independent review, and develop a plain-language public summary (UNESCO, 2021; NIST, 2023; Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat, n.d.).

3.4 Lessons for policy transfer

Bangladesh can adopt functions from these instruments while designing institutions for its own labor market. A representative consultation rule cannot depend solely on the presence of a formal works council. An appeal process cannot assume that a platform worker can reach an employer in person. A public register should explain the decision and complaint route without exposing personal information. The design must account for informal employment, limited enforcement capacity, accessibility, and the practical cost of obtaining a remedy.

4 Bangladesh Context and Proposed Legislation

4.1 Institutional starting point

The Planning Commission’s General Economics Division reports that roughly 84 percent of employment was informal in 2024, based on Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics labor force data. A policy that relies entirely on formal payroll records or established enterprise representation would leave many affected people difficult to reach. Fairwork’s Bangladesh platform assessments provide local evidence about conditions on selected platforms. The sample offers cases for inquiry and cannot establish conditions for every platform or the effect of any particular algorithm (GED, 2025; Fairwork, 2023).

Bangladesh already has relevant legal instruments. The official record includes the Personal Data Protection Act, 2026, and the Bangladesh Labour (Amendment) Act, 2026. The Bangladesh Public Procurement Authority also documents the adoption of Public Procurement Rules, 2025. Before drafting clauses, government counsel should prepare a section-by-section map of the consolidated labor, data, procurement, and sectoral rules, including commencement and available remedies. This thesis verifies the existence of those instruments and leaves the detailed compatibility assessment for legislative drafting (Bangladesh Parliament, 2026a, 2026b; BPPA, 2025).

4.2 Coverage and proportional duties

The proposed law would cover public bodies and private organizations when they use AI or comparable automated systems to materially influence decisions about employment, earnings, essential services, or public benefits. The law should state coverage for applicants, contractors, and platform workers explicitly. It should define when a system materially influences a decision, including when a nominal human reviewer routinely follows its recommendation.

Low-risk assistive tools would follow ordinary privacy, security, and procurement rules. High-risk uses would require documented evaluation, notice, monitoring, and a remedy. Independent pre-deployment review would apply to the most consequential uses, such as systems that can deny a public benefit, screen applicants out of work at scale, or effectively terminate access to platform income. A targeted prohibition should address specified practices where safeguards cannot reasonably prevent serious rights violations. Drafting should define those practices and any exceptions narrowly.

4.3 Duties, institutions, and enforcement

The deploying organization should be accountable for the decision it makes. Providers should supply documentation, cooperate with authorized inspection, and notify deployers of changes affecting the approved use. Contracts should guarantee regulator access to records where necessary. Trade secrets can justify controlled access to technical material, while affected people should still receive reasons sufficient to challenge a decision.

Assign a lead authority to publish common standards, coordinate inspections, and maintain the public register. Labor and sector regulators should investigate cases in their areas under clearly assigned powers. An independent appeal route should remain available when a deployer rejects a complaint. Review panels should disclose conflicts and recuse conflicted members. Regulators should publish aggregate data on complaints, remedies, and compliance. Remedies can include correction, reconsideration, orders to change a practice, and suspension, with sanctions proportionate to harm, scale, and recurrence.

4.4 Worker voice and shared gains

Employers should disclose planned uses before material changes to work take effect. Worker representatives should be able to inspect the evidence, seek technical assistance, and bargain over changes to pay, quotas, hours, staffing, and surveillance. A platform-work procedure should provide ways to choose representatives and raise issues across dispersed workers. Consultation should have a response deadline and an anti-retaliation safeguard.

Where firms claim productivity gains, representatives should be able to verify how those gains were calculated. Net gains should account for software and integration costs, human review, errors, and rework. Output per hour should be read alongside injury rates, workload, actual hours, and earnings. Bargaining outcomes can include shorter paid hours, wage increases, transition training, or bonuses. The policy should protect existing wage and safety floors throughout the transition.

4.5 Public procurement and shared capacity

Government should publish a register of high-risk deployments with the purpose, accountable agency, assessment summary, and complaint channel. Preserve an accessible human route for essential services. Require government contracts to provide audit access, advance notice of material updates, limits on secondary use of public data, data portability, and a right to suspend unsafe deployments. Evaluate bids using the cost of implementation, review, and switching alongside the purchase price.

Fund Bangla-language evaluation datasets, accessibility testing, and shared computing for universities and smaller firms. Document dataset provenance, language coverage, reuse rights, and privacy safeguards. Let cooperatives and worker-owned projects compete under published grant and procurement criteria. Distinguish recurring costs for enforcement and training from capital spending. Pilot shared resources before committing to infrastructure whose demand and operating costs remain uncertain.

5 High Risk AI Deployment Protocol

A protocol should apply to a defined organization, purpose, population, workflow, and system version. The responsible manager should sign it. Where required, an independent reviewer should issue a written determination. The organization should publish an accessible summary for public deployments and share appropriate information with affected people and worker representatives.

Independent reviewers should have access to appropriate data, the evaluation plan and results, and any unresolved objections. Where the evidence is weak or subgroup data are sparse, they can restrict the use, request further testing, or refuse approval. Worker representatives should be able to contest how measures such as productivity or work intensity are defined. The appeal function should preserve enough information to reconstruct the decision without indefinitely retaining personal data.

6 Two Applications

6.1 Garment inspection and production quotas

Suppose a factory introduces machine vision to identify defects. The system’s stated purpose is quality inspection. If management later uses the output to raise quotas or score workers, that changes how the system affects labor. The protocol should identify both uses separately. The inspection test can measure missed defects, false alarms, rework, and performance across lines and materials. The labor evaluation can measure overtime, work intensity, earnings, injuries, and how jobs change across shifts.

A possible evaluation would compare similar production lines before and after introduction, accounting for product mix and seasonality. If safety worsens or error rates exceed the predefined limits, the responsible manager should pause the affected use. Worker representatives should have access to the evidence before negotiations over new quotas. Independent technical approval would not itself authorize changes in the terms of work.

6.2 Automated screening for a public benefit

Suppose an agency introduces a tool to prioritize applications for review. The main risk is that an eligible applicant could wait longer or be wrongly denied because of incomplete records or an inaccessible interface. The agency should begin by documenting the existing service process and its errors. A shadow pilot could compare recommendations with independent human assessments, using a sample that includes cases the system ranks low.

During any live pilot, the agency should preserve an accessible human route, issue understandable reasons for adverse decisions, and make appeals available without requiring advanced digital skills. An oversight body should track wrongful exclusions, time to decision, successful appeals, and access across language, disability, and location where data collection is lawful and appropriate. The pilot should pause if access deteriorates beyond a predefined threshold or if a serious privacy incident occurs.

7 Implementation and Evaluation

7.1 First 100 days and first year

In the first 100 days, inventory high-risk government systems and identify the contracts, agencies, and data flows involved. Publish an interim procurement standard within existing legal authority. Release a consultation paper and hold sessions with unions, platform workers, people with disabilities, employers, researchers, and organizations working outside Dhaka. Publish a response explaining which recommendations the government adopts and why.

In the first year, publish draft legislation, cost the oversight and appeals functions, and assign responsibilities to named authorities. Prioritize funding for reviewers, labor inspectors, public-sector staff, and accessible grievance channels. Pilot the protocol in one public service and one workplace setting before expansion. Procure Bangla-language evaluation resources with documented licensing and privacy rules. Budget decisions should distinguish recurring enforcement costs from time-limited investment in infrastructure and training.

7.2 Empirical research design

The core empirical question is whether AI adoption with the proposed safeguards improves productivity while protecting access and working conditions. A pilot could follow workers or applicants before and after deployment and compare outcomes with comparable sites that have yet to adopt. Where rollout timing can be assigned without affecting rights or withholding essential services, randomized rollout can improve causal inference. Where adoption is staggered by operational choice, estimate cohort-specific changes and examine whether pre-adoption trends are comparable. If a credible comparison is unavailable, report descriptive changes and explain competing causes.

Define the unit of analysis in advance. Worker-level outcomes include effective hourly earnings, hours, job transitions, and injuries. Site-level outcomes include output quality, rework, staffing, and complaints. Applicant-level outcomes include time to decision, exclusion errors, and successful appeals. For platform workers, effective hourly earnings should account for necessary expenses and unpaid waiting time. Report uncertainty and use methods that account for clustering at the level of assignment or adoption.

Quantitative evaluation should be paired with confidential interviews with workers, applicants, managers, and reviewers. People who lose access to a platform or leave employment should be included where feasible because observing only active workers can hide harms. For subgroup estimates, report sample sizes and uncertainty. Suppress or aggregate very small cells when disclosure risks outweigh their value. Evaluation teams should preregister outcomes, define stopping rules and a privacy plan, and publish results even when they are unfavorable.

7.3 Limitations

The comparative cases show available policy instruments and do not establish that their effects will transfer to Bangladesh. Local estimates of AI adoption, subcontracting, and algorithmic decisions remain incomplete. Formal complaints may rise because people gain access to a remedy. Low complaint counts can reflect fear, poor information, or administrative barriers. Early pilots should therefore measure awareness of rights and the usability of complaint channels as well as the number of filings.

8 Conclusion

A government can support useful AI while requiring stronger evidence and accountability where automated decisions affect livelihoods or essential services. The proposed allocation of authority gives independent reviewers responsibility for evaluating evidence, gives workers a voice in changes to their working conditions, and gives affected people a route to correction and remedy. For Bangladesh, the practical test is whether these rights reach people in informal work and those who cannot readily navigate a digital or legal process. The first pilots should measure those outcomes before the framework expands.
