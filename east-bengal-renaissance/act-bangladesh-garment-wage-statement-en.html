ACT Brands’ Statement on Garment Wages: Turn Support into Commitments

Editorial statement by East Bengal Renaissance · November 23, 2026

We welcome the recent joint statement by ACT member brands supporting an urgent and meaningful increase in the minimum wage for Bangladesh’s ready-made garment workers. The brands say wage improvements should reflect workers’ living costs, inflation, productivity, and relevant economic conditions. They also call for the protection of workers’ rights to organize, assemble peacefully, and take part in social dialogue.

This support matters because Bangladesh’s garment industry is not only a matter of factories and exports. It is tied to the livelihoods of millions of people, particularly the work and family security of a large number of women workers. But policy support becomes meaningful only when it is reflected in wage-setting and purchasing practices.

Support must be specific

ACT’s statement supports a wage increase but names no amount. It should therefore not be presented as full support for any specific wage demand made by workers. The Minimum Wage Board should examine evidence on workers’ and families’ living needs, inflation, productivity, employment, and the sustainability of the industry. Its decision should make real progress toward an income earned during regular working hours that supports a dignified standard of living.

In an earlier proposal, East Bengal Renaissance presented living-cost and international purchasing-price calculations in support of a Tk 30,000 monthly minimum wage for the garment sector. That is our own proposal; ACT’s statement does not endorse this figure. Read our wage proposal.

Who will bear the cost of higher wages?

Recognizing the need for higher wages while leaving the entire cost to factories or workers does not solve the problem. International brands acknowledge their role in supply chains and say their purchasing practices should help deliver higher wages and living wages, and that negotiated wage increases should be incorporated into order prices. To make this commitment real, brands should disclose how, by how much, and on what timeline they will adjust prices paid to suppliers.

Without transparent order pricing, the pressure created by a wage increase can fall on the weakest parties in the supply chain. Discussions among the Board, brands, suppliers, government, and worker representatives should therefore address purchasing prices, production costs, and the practical capacity to pay wages.

Safety, the right to organize, and a transparent process

Disagreement over wages or workers’ demands must not be used to justify violence, intimidation, retaliation, harassment, or restrictions on the right to organize. Allegations should also be assessed on specific evidence and through a fair process. Trust requires effective participation by worker representatives, a clear timetable for negotiations, public information about the evidence behind decisions, and regular wage reviews.

Our call

We call on the Minimum Wage Board and all relevant parties to reach a timely, transparent, evidence-based agreement that ensures equal and meaningful participation by worker representatives. International brands should move beyond statements of principle and publish measurable commitments explaining how their purchase prices will cover wage increases. Workers’ living standards and the industry’s long-term sustainability should not be set against each other; solutions require fair bargaining and shared responsibility.
