What Could the Minimum Wage for Garment Workers Be by District or Division?

East Bengal Renaissance · 23 November 2026

Does the same wage ensure the same standard of living in Dhaka, Gazipur, Narayanganj, Chattogram or Rangpur? Comparing rents alone will not answer the question. Food, transport, healthcare, children’s education, family size and the number of earners in a household all matter. This article combines the Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics’ (BBS) regional poverty lines from 2022 with the Centre for Policy Dialogue’s (CPD) 2026 living-wage estimate to show a preliminary divisional scenario.

The short answer: This simplified calculation produces monthly figures of roughly Tk 15,300 to Tk 24,200 depending on division and rural or urban location. These numbers are not a final wage recommendation. A government district-level schedule should not be announced without checking local prices, especially rent and transport costs.

What figures are in the national debate?

The 2023 minimum wage for the ready-made garment sector was set at Tk 12,500. In 2026, CPD presented three different benchmarks. Adjusting for inflation brings the purchasing-power equivalent of Tk 12,500 to Tk 16,351. CPD estimates that one earner would need Tk 18,578 to keep a family of four above Dhaka’s urban upper poverty line. Its living-wage estimate is Tk 24,204 per earner, based on food, other essentials, rent and a small emergency reserve. That estimate assumes a four-person family with 1.58 earners. CPD’s full calculations and assumptions

These figures do not mean the same thing. Tk 16,351 restores the purchasing power of the old wage but does not improve living standards. Tk 18,578 is a poverty-line benchmark. Tk 24,204 is CPD’s estimate for a basic but decent life in Dhaka. None should be treated as a measured cost-of-living figure for every district in the country.

How was the divisional calculation made?

BBS’s HIES 2022 report provides an upper poverty line (UPL) for rural and urban areas in each division. Dhaka’s urban UPL was Tk 4,922 per person per month. Using this as a comparison point, the calculation applies a simple ratio:

Indicative local wage = Tk 24,204 × (local UPL ÷ Dhaka urban UPL of Tk 4,922)

Where this result is below Tk 18,578, the table also shows a scenario with CPD’s Dhaka poverty-line wage equivalent as a national reference floor. This second step is an illustrative policy scenario, not an official proposal. It shows one way to prevent an area’s wage from falling below the same national reference simply because the local poverty-line ratio is lower.

The BBS UPL is not a living wage. It is a basic-needs poverty threshold that incorporates some geographic price variation. Applying its ratio to CPD’s 2026 Dhaka living-wage estimate produces a comparison scenario, not a complete local price index. Figures are rounded to the nearest taka.

How should we read these results?

First, the calculation shows differences between rural and urban areas within the same division. The urban UPL ratios for Rangpur and Sylhet produce higher estimates than their rural ratios. Dhaka’s urban estimate is the highest. But these differences are not a complete picture of garment workers’ budgets. Factory location, workers’ residential areas, daily commuting and rent can vary even within one district.

Second, it would be wrong to use poverty rates as a cost-of-living index. BBS’s Poverty Map 2022 gives model-based estimates of how many people live below the poverty line in each district and upazila. It is not a district price index or a table of the wages workers need. A higher poverty rate does not by itself mean that prices are higher in that area. BBS, World Bank and WFP Poverty Map

Third, the simplified calculation has substantial limitations. CPD’s Tk 24,204 estimate uses Dhaka district food prices from 2025, inflation through October 2026, an HIES expenditure ratio and an assumed rent of Tk 10,000. Scaling it by 2022 regional poverty-line ratios does not separately measure local 2026 rent, commuting, healthcare or childcare. The result also changes if family size or the number of earners changes.

What would a fair district or divisional system look like?

The available data are not enough to produce a reliable wage schedule for all 64 districts. BBS would need to collect regular local market prices for industrial zones and workers’ residential areas. It should separately measure the cost of a four-person worker household’s food basket, rent, utilities, healthcare, education, transport, clothing and emergency savings. A representative worker survey should also estimate how many people in a household actually earn.

A stronger design would set a national minimum wage, with transparent supplements in high-cost areas. Wages should not be reduced because one area is cheaper; local indexes should raise wages where they show additional costs. The index, data, sample, calculation formula and uncertainty should all be published. ILO Convention No. 131 likewise calls for transparent, evidence-based wage setting that considers workers’ and families’ needs, cost of living and inflation, alongside productivity, employment and the sustainability of enterprises. ILO statement on Bangladesh’s wage review, 19 November 2026

So the research-based answer is not one magic number. Available data allow us to test a comparative divisional framework between Tk 18,578 and Tk 24,204; the raw ratio outside Dhaka can fall as low as Tk 15,284, which shows why a poverty-line ratio should not be used as the final wage. To secure a dignified life, policymakers first need to set an adequate national base wage. Only then should updated, public local cost-of-living data determine any necessary upward adjustment.
