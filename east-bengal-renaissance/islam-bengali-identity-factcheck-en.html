“Islam made us Bengali”: a historical fact-check

An image attributed to Farhad Mazhar says that Islam did not make Bengalis Arab, Iranian, or Turkic; it made them Bengali. One reading of this statement fits the historical record. Another makes history too simple.

East Bengal Renaissance · Historical fact-check · November 24, 2026

Verdict: Partly true, but incomplete as a historical account. Islam has deeply shaped the language, literature, society, and regional identity of Bengali Muslims. But the historical record does not support the claim that Islam began Bengali language or regional culture. The image also gives no original speech, text, date, or page reference, so the exact wording cannot be verified from the image alone.

First the quote, then the history

The image attributes these words to Farhad Mazhar: “Islam did not make us Arab; Islam did not make us Iranian; Islam did not make us Turkic; Islam made us Bengali.” But it provides no link to the original interview, speech, essay, date of publication, or full context. We therefore refer to it as a statement attributed to him in the image. Without a reliable original record, its exact wording and context cannot be confirmed.

There are two separate questions here: did Farhad Mazhar say these exact words, and how persuasive is the historical meaning of the statement? The second can be discussed through historical scholarship; the first requires the original source.

Did Bengal have its own language and culture before Islam?

Yes, although it would be misleading to imagine people in the distant past as a single, fixed “Bengali nation” in the modern sense. The verses of the Charyapada, often considered the earliest surviving evidence of Bangla, are generally dated to the ninth through eleventh centuries. They contain features shared with Bangla, Odia, Assamese, and Bihari. Local languages and cultural traditions were developing in the region long before the political expansion of Islam. The Charyapada is not modern standard Bangla, but it is an important earlier layer in the language’s history. [2]

“Bengal” in this period did not mean the territory of today’s Bangladesh or a modern national identity. Regional, linguistic, and political boundaries changed over many centuries. We should neither project modern identity unchanged into the past nor claim that the region had no local language or culture before Islam arrived.

What did Islam change in Bengal?

Muslim political presence in Bengal grew with the expansion of Turkish rule in the early thirteenth century. But the establishment of rule and the conversion of ordinary people were not the same event. Islamization in Bengal was a long and uneven process. Richard Eaton’s research shows that political expansion, agricultural growth, new settlements, Sufi shrines, and village mosques interacted; no single cause can explain the development of Bengali Muslim society. [1]

Islam profoundly influenced Bengali society and culture. Arabic and Persian words entered Bangla; Muslim poets and writers created literature; religious learning circulated through manuscripts and other institutions; and Sufi traditions, mosques, khanqahs, festivals, and local practices shaped everyday life. Islam took on a regional character as it became part of local languages and social experience. In the colonial era, Bengali Muslim cultural and political identity was discussed and articulated more explicitly through language, literature, and print. [3]

In this sense, if “Islam made us Bengali” means that Bengali Muslims received and practiced Islam through local language, society, and culture, the statement points to an important historical truth. It would diminish Bengal’s history to treat Islam only as a copy of an external Arab or Iranian culture.

Where does the statement overreach?

If the statement means that Islam alone created the Bengali language, Bengali people, or all of Bengal’s culture, it is not established history. The older roots of Bangla reach back before Islamization, and Bengali culture emerged through the long interaction of many religious, linguistic, and social traditions. Bengali Muslim identity is an important part of Bengali identity, but it is not the only part. Hindu, Buddhist, Christian, and Indigenous communities are also part of Bengal’s languages, cultures, and history.

These identities do not cancel one another out. A person can be Bengali and Muslim at the same time; being Muslim is not a condition for belonging to Bangla language or culture. Religious identity, language, region, class, ethnicity, and political experience sometimes reinforce one another and sometimes create tensions. The task of history is to understand that complexity, not to make one identity the sole source of another.

Conclusion

The statement in the image points to an important historical insight: Islam did not remain in Bengal as an unchanged foreign template. People in Bengal received, reshaped, and practiced it through their own languages and social experiences. But saying that “Islam created the Bengalis” obscures the older history of Bangla, Bengal’s layered culture, and the plurality of its identities. A more precise conclusion is: Islam has been one of the major historical forces shaping Bengali Muslim society and identity; it is not the sole source of Bengali language and culture.
