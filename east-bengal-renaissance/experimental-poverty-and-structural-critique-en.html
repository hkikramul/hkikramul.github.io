Testing Poverty: What RCTs Tell Us—and Which Structural Questions Remain

The contribution and limits of the experimental approach to development economics associated with Abhijit Banerjee, Esther Duflo, and Michael Kremer, read through Marxian political economy.

Original essay and central argument: Anupam Shanto
Edited and source-checked version: East Bengal Renaissance · November 3, 2026

What was the Nobel Prize for?

The 2019 Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel was awarded jointly to Abhijit Banerjee, Esther Duflo, and Michael Kremer “for their experimental approach to alleviating global poverty.” The award recognized their contribution to using field experiments to measure the effects of specific interventions and to building this research approach into development economics. It was not an award for discovering a single, comprehensive theory of poverty. [1]

This distinction matters. A strength of their work is breaking a broad policy question into smaller questions that can be tested with data: Does a reliable vaccination camp increase uptake? What changes when a small incentive is added? How much do students learn when extra teaching support is provided?

What an experiment can show

In a randomized controlled trial, eligible people, households, schools, or locations are assigned by chance to groups that receive or do not receive a program. When the study is well designed and implemented, the average difference in outcomes between groups can provide a credible estimate of the causal effect of that specific program. The estimate must still be interpreted within the boundaries of the population, time period, intervention, and outcome that were studied.

In a trial in rural Rajasthan, India, the rate of full immunization was 6% in control villages, 18% where reliable monthly immunization camps were held, and 39% where the camps also offered small incentives such as lentils and metal plates. The finding shows that reliable services and modest incentives can increase uptake. But 39% still meant that most children were not fully immunized; the trial did not establish that every structural problem in the health system had been solved. [2]

In a Kenyan experiment on insecticide-treated malaria nets, uptake fell by about 75% when the price rose from zero to $0.75. The researchers found no evidence that women who received free nets were less likely to use them than women who paid. This is evidence about the relationship between price and uptake in a specific setting; it does not, by itself, establish that the same result will hold for other products, countries, or long-term policies. [3]

Two experiments in Indian schools found that remedial teaching for students who were falling behind and computer-assisted mathematics instruction improved test scores. Some initial gains had faded a year later. So the question is not only whether a program works, but also how long it works, for whom, and how it can be sustained within regular schools. [4]

A Marxian question: how is poverty reproduced?

These findings can be valuable for policy. A Marxian critique need not dismiss them; it asks which questions become central to research and which recede into the background. RCTs typically test how a defined intervention—such as cash, credit, incentives, training, or service delivery—changes behavior and measurable outcomes. Whether poverty is only a problem of poor decisions or inadequate access to services is a separate theoretical question.

Marxian political economy asks: Who owns the means of production? How much of the value produced by workers returns to them as wages, and who controls the surplus? How do workers’ bargaining power, land ownership, credit relations, the pressure to accumulate and profit, and state policy shape class relations and income distribution? A small-scale average treatment effect alone cannot answer these questions.

The point of critique, then, is the selection of research questions and the unit of analysis. If poverty is treated mainly as a problem of household behavior, incentives, or access to services, relations of production, ownership of assets, and unequal power may move into the background. This approach can identify effective programs while leaving incomplete an account of how poverty may be generated or reproduced through social and economic relations. This is a theoretical critique of a research tradition, not an evidence-based claim about the researchers’ personal intentions.

Statistical strengths and limits

Random assignment strengthens comparisons within the chosen sample. It does not determine which problem should be studied, which outcome should count as success, or how markets and institutions outside the trial will respond. When a result from one locality is applied nationally, supply systems, labor markets, prices, budgets, and people’s responses may change. These general-equilibrium effects and longer-term changes require separate analysis.

Implementation quality, consent to participate, sample representativeness, the timing of outcome measurement, interactions with other programs, and spillovers to comparison groups can all affect an estimate. RCTs are a powerful design for estimating causal effects, but they do not automatically establish generalizability, explain every mechanism, or provide a complete theory of the economy. Angus Deaton’s methodological review and Martin Ravallion’s review of Poor Economics both discuss the need to connect experiments with theory, context, and broader evidence. [5] [6]

Microcredit: neither a miracle cure nor a universal failure

A synthesis of six randomized evaluations of microcredit described the average effects as “modestly positive, but not transformative.” Results varied across settings. Access to loans could affect business activity or some household decisions, but the evidence did not show that microcredit, on average, eradicates poverty. So it is an overstatement to say that nobody benefited anywhere; it is also beyond the evidence to claim that providing microcredit will, by itself, end poverty. [7]

A Marxian reading asks what comes next: Does credit change ownership of productive assets, or does it make poor households more dependent on debt within existing markets? Interest rates, lender power, borrowers’ income risks, and who bears the cost of failure also matter. We can recognize that access to credit may help in particular circumstances while observing that credit alone does not change class relations or the distribution of wealth.

From critique to the next research questions

This approach is especially useful when there is a clear causal question about a specific program or policy. Structural questions require it to be combined with labor-market and wage data, evidence on wealth and ownership, long-term panel data, historical research, qualitative inquiry, and political-economy analysis. Research can measure not only average income or service uptake, but also whose income rose, who controlled the surplus, who bore the risks and costs, and whether bargaining power changed after an intervention.

It is not supported by evidence to describe the work of Banerjee, Duflo, and Kremer as “praise for capitalism” or a conscious project to preserve poverty. A stronger critique is that their experimental approach provides credible knowledge about the effectiveness of particular interventions, while the class relations and production relations that shape poverty are often not its primary unit of analysis. The more productive debate is to bring RCT findings into conversation with a broader Marxian political economy, rather than to dismiss the method outright.
