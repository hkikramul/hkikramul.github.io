“If Workers Can Live, Industry Can Survive”: A Slogan, Its Logic, and Its Politics

How workers’ lives, wages, and rights are linked to industrial sustainability: a reading of a quote card attributed to Taslima Akhter and a caption attributed to Firoz Ahmed.

Original essay and analysis: Anupam Shaikat Shanto
Editing, source review, and English translation: East Bengal Renaissance · November 3, 2026

বাংলা সংস্করণ পড়ুন ↗

First, a note on the sources

The source essay quotes a circulating card attributing this statement to Taslima Akhter (referred to as “Lima apa” in the essay): “If workers can live, industry can survive. Not only industry, but the country will survive.” The essay also quotes a Facebook caption attributed to Firoz Ahmed. We could not directly verify the original quote card, its full broadcast context, or Ahmed’s original post for this edition. The analysis below therefore examines the wording and caption as supplied by the author; it does not present them as independently verified full statements.

News reports about a video message by Akhter on November 2 describe her as saying that wage increases were necessary while also urging workers to be patient and warning against attempts to divert the movement. Those reports summarize her remarks; phrases such as “government press-release language” or “defending employers’ interests” are interpretations, not independently verifiable quotations. [1]

Five sentences, five different claims

  1. “If industry survives, workers survive.”
  2. “If workers can live, industry can survive.”
  3. “Politics that protects industry in order to save workers.”
  4. “Politics that protects workers in order to save industry.”
  5. “Reckless politics that destroys industry in the name of saving workers.”

The first two sentences are not mirror images. In logical terms, “if industry continues, workers will have jobs” is a different causal claim from “if workers’ lives and working conditions improve, industry will be sustainable over time.” Both may hold under some conditions, but neither is automatically true for every industry, worker, or policy.

There is another distinction: having a job is not the same as living with dignity. A worker may remain employed while earning too little, working in unsafe conditions, relying on excessive overtime, or lacking the right to organize. The survival of a factory therefore does not by itself establish that workers’ rights and living standards are protected.

Mutual dependence—and a conflict over whose interests come first

Industrial production requires labour, skill, health, and experience. Workers’ ability to live and reproduce their labour power requires income, security, and social protection. Wages and working conditions are therefore not only immediate production costs; they are also part of workers’ lives and of the long-term capacity to produce.

Still, “if workers can live, industry can survive” should not be treated as a universal law without evidence. Industrial survival also depends on productivity, energy and credit costs, orders, prices paid by international buyers, technology, management, and public policy. Workers’ wages and rights matter, but they are not the only determinants. The ILO recommends setting wages through an evidence-based process that considers workers’ needs together with employment, productivity, and business conditions. [2]

From a Marxian political-economy perspective, another question is how the value produced and the surplus are distributed, and who bears the pressure to raise wages or cut production costs. When a demand for fair wages is made, will it be deferred in the name of “saving industry”? That is not only an economic calculation; it is also a question of power and bargaining. At the same time, the real costs of production and the sustainability of employment cannot be ignored.

Firoz Ahmed’s reading: contradiction or a debate over strategy?

In Shanto’s essay, a caption attributed to Firoz Ahmed interprets “if workers can live, industry can survive” as a politics of “protecting industry in order to save workers,” and contrasts it with what it calls “reckless politics” that saves workers by destroying industry. This interpretation does not follow as a matter of simple logic from the quoted slogan; it is a political reading. The sentence itself does not say that saving workers requires protecting owners’ profits or preserving the existing industrial structure first.

But calling it a direct contradiction would also be too quick. Ahmed may mean that strategies for winning workers’ rights should avoid needlessly endangering both their livelihoods and production. That can be a coherent policy position. The problem is that terms such as “reckless” or “destroying industry” need to be tied to specific actions and evidence. Otherwise they can replace analysis with a moral label.

The useful questions are: Are demands for wages, safety, or union rights being treated as anti-industry by definition? Or is the discussion about specific tactics such as arson, vandalism, or preventing others from working? Peaceful strikes and collective bargaining should not be lumped together with violence or property destruction. Allegations of actual violence should likewise be assessed separately and on specific evidence.

What the verifiable wage context shows

News reports in November 2026 state that the current monthly minimum wage for garment workers is Tk 12,500, while workers’ ongoing demands include a Tk 30,000 minimum. On November 1, the government formed a wage board to determine a new minimum wage. [3] [4]

The source essay also refers to a BGMEA analysis allegedly finding that a Tk 20,000 minimum wage would close most factories and cost about four million workers their jobs. We could not locate the underlying BGMEA document or its methodology, so we do not present that estimate as an established fact. Separately, a report citing BGMEA figures said that 318 factories were closed and more than 700,000 workers were unable to work during the labour unrest on November 1. This reports work stoppages during the protests; it is not evidence that implementing a Tk 20,000 wage would permanently close factories. [5]

To assess the effects of a wage proposal, analysis would need to consider factory-level productivity and profits, buyer prices, energy and financing costs, employment, and workers’ cost of living together. “A wage increase will destroy industry” and “a wage increase will automatically make industry sustainable” are both overgeneralizations without evidence. ILO guidance also emphasizes balancing workers’ needs with economic conditions, social dialogue, and reliable data. [2] [6]

The central question: whose interests are prioritized?

The political difference between “if industry survives, workers survive” and “if workers can live, industry can survive” is not only the order of the words. The key questions are who bears the costs of keeping industry operating, and how much weight decisions give to workers’ lives, wages, safety, and right to organize.

Shanto’s question about how Ahmed’s caption interprets the quote card is worth discussing. But without the original card and full context, we cannot determine anyone’s intent. A stronger critique points to the exact wording, the steps in the argument, and the evidence—then asks which reading puts workers’ interests first, and which uses the language of industrial survival to push those interests aside.
