Waz, Public Life and Foreign Serials: Three Research Drafts

Literature reviews, research questions and preliminary designs

These are preliminary research proposals, not completed studies or predetermined conclusions. They are offered for discussion by East Bengal Renaissance and do not represent an official position of any political organization or religious institution.

Religious preaching and entertainment serials can be connected to people’s emotions, social relationships, cultural identities and economic choices. These proposals do not assume in advance that the effects are either beneficial or harmful. They ask how audiences experience the content and how those experiences relate to social and economic contexts.

1. Psychological and social experiences of Islamic waz · 2. The economic market around waz mahfils · 3. Foreign serials and audience experience

1. Psychological and Social Experiences of Islamic Waz

Rationale

Waz mahfils are settings for religious instruction and advice, but also for public gathering, emotionally performed speech, social interaction and online discussion. The purpose is not to label waz as a cause of mental illness. It is to examine how different listeners interpret sermons, what they feel, and how those experiences relate to everyday life.

Brief literature review

  • Stille (2021) analyzes how voice, rhythm, humour and rhetorical form convey emotion in Bangladeshi Islamic preaching. He connects emotion with public participation, moral imagination and political subjectivity rather than treating it only as a private feeling. The article does not directly measure audience outcomes through a survey.
  • Rifat, Amin and Ahmed (2022) analyze the topics and performances in 50 YouTube sermon videos. They show how preachers use religious and cultural elements to engage audience emotions, and how online sermons enter social and political discussion. Video analysis alone cannot establish causal effects on viewers.
  • Stille’s book (2020) treats waz as an important site for understanding public piety, performance and public discourse in Bangladesh.
  • Al-Zaman (2022) analyzes metadata for 73,120 Islamic YouTube videos from 2011–2020, studying the growth of online Islamic content and user engagement. It maps the digital environment; it does not measure waz listeners’ psychological outcomes.

Research gap

Existing work provides an important foundation on rhetoric, video content, emotional performance and digital circulation. More direct research is needed on how listeners across age, gender, education, urban-rural location and levels of participation interpret these experiences. Potential outcomes should be distinguished carefully: calm, hope, meaning, social connection, fear, guilt, anger, or attitudes toward disagreement are not interchangeable measures.

Preliminary research draft

Working title: Islamic Waz in Bangladeshi Public Life: A Mixed-Methods Study of Emotional Experience, Social Connection and Moral Reflection.

Main question: How are experiences of listening to different forms of waz related to listeners’ emotions, social connection, moral reflection and everyday decisions?

  • What feelings and meanings do listeners seek or report during and after a sermon?
  • How are sermon topic, performance, prior beliefs and social context related to those experiences?
  • How do in-person mahfils compare with online listening?
  • Which positive or negative emotions appear in listeners’ own accounts, and how long do they believe those experiences last?

Design: Begin with semi-structured interviews with 30–40 listeners from varied backgrounds. Use those interviews to refine culturally appropriate questions for a survey of approximately 400–600 adults across several districts and urban-rural settings. If feasible, a short diary component could record listening context and before-after self-reported emotions. Final sample size and instruments should follow piloting, resources and ethics review.

Measures and analysis: Record listening patterns, topics, duration, setting and listeners’ interpretations. Use validated Bangla measures of emotional well-being or social connection where appropriate; otherwise translate, back-translate and pilot the items. Self-report is not a clinical diagnosis. A survey can identify associations, while causal claims require a longitudinal or comparative design.

Ethics: Religious identity and practice must not be treated as symptoms of illness. Participation should be voluntary, identities protected, quotation of sensitive statements consent-based, and political or sectarian risks minimized. Institutional ethics review should precede data collection.

2. The Economic Market Around Islamic Waz Mahfils

Rationale

Organizing and circulating a mahfil may involve speakers and committees as well as sound, staging, transport, food, recording, editing, video channels and online promotion. Reliable public information about funding, costs, compensation and digital revenue-sharing is limited. This study would not assess the theological truth of sermons; it would examine labor, money flows and media production.

Brief literature review

  • Rifat, Prottoy and Ahmed (2022) conducted a nine-month ethnographic study of waz video production, sharing and viewing. Participants described small media firms, low-cost equipment, travel expenses, video sales and efforts to build audiences on YouTube. This offers a valuable map of the production ecology, but does not estimate total national revenue or profit.
  • Al-Zaman (2022) used metadata from a large collection of YouTube videos to analyze the growth and engagement of Islamic content. This helps describe the platform context but does not measure actual channel revenue, expenses or workers’ wages.
  • Ahmed (2024) examines the shift of waz mahfil from physical gathering spaces to YouTube’s digital space. The study maps circulation and reach rather than the full economics of the market.

Research gap

Public discussion can focus on a popular preacher’s fee or a viral video’s possible earnings. Less is known about the full chain: committees and donors, local businesses, speakers and assistants, sound and stage workers, media crews, video rights, advertising, sponsorship and platform revenue. Seasonal work, unpaid labor, financial risk and unequal distribution also need investigation.

Preliminary research draft

Working title: The Economy of Waz Mahfils: A Value-Chain Study of Event Organization, Preacher Labor and Digital Content Markets in Bangladesh.

Main question: How are money, labor, rights and revenue organized and distributed from local mahfils to digital video platforms?

  • What are the main funding sources and cost categories for mahfils?
  • How are work and compensation organized for speakers, committees, sound and stage workers, camera crews and editors?
  • Who controls video ownership, exclusive rights, advertising, sponsorship, donations and platform income?
  • How do seasonality, popularity, location and digital skills shape earnings and job security?

Design: Conduct event-level cost and revenue case studies of 25–30 mahfils in urban and rural settings across three districts; confidential interviews with 15–20 organizing committees, 15–20 media/video firms, and a combined 60–80 speakers, workers, sponsors and donors; and a non-invasive analysis of public information from 30–50 YouTube channels. These are initial targets and should be revised after access assessment and piloting.

Analysis: Map the value chain for each event and video, recording cost categories, revenue sources, labor hours and risks. Public view counts must not be treated as income. Revenue shares, fees and honoraria should only be reported as voluntarily supplied or verifiable information. Estimating a national market total requires a representative sample and additional data.

Ethics: Protect private information about income, fees and donations. Do not record or identify participants without consent. Analyze labor and market relations without devaluing religious service.

3. Foreign Serials and Bangladeshi Audiences: Emotion, Social Expectations and Consumption

Rationale

Several studies in Bangladesh address Indian Bangla and Hindi television serials. Less research compares audience experience across Turkish, Korean, Pakistani and streaming-based foreign serials. Rather than repeating broad claims that “foreign culture changes people,” research can ask why viewers choose particular content, how they relate to characters and stories, and how viewing experiences are connected to relationships, aspirations, identity or consumption.

Brief literature review

  • Razu, Yeasmin and Islam (2018) interviewed 60 purposively selected respondents in Dhaka and Khulna about Indian serials, lifestyle and family relationships. The study raises perceived concerns, but its small purposive sample and reliance on reported perceptions cannot prove causal effects.
  • Bhowmick and Sharief (2020) examine why Bangladeshi audiences watch Indian soap operas. They discuss narrative, characterization, women’s roles and audience preferences, alongside limitations in local television production.
  • Tarannum and Siddiqa (2024) use uses-and-gratifications, cultivation and social-learning theories in a qualitative study of 100 women aged 18–25 at Khulna University. Because the sample is limited to one university group, it cannot represent all viewers; it also does not clinically measure mental health.
  • Bangladeshi consumer research has examined purchase intention and behavior for Indian fashion products in relation to drama serials. Tunna (2023) studies public perceptions of the cultural effects of Indian satellite programming.

Research gap

Existing work largely centers on Indian serials, small samples and audience perceptions. “Psychological impact” should not be equated with clinical harm. Researchers should distinguish emotional response, identification with characters, relationship expectations, identity, aspirations, language use, clothing or product preferences, and time use. Age, gender, class, education, location, access to local content and platform choice may all matter.

Preliminary research draft

Working title: Foreign Serial Viewing in Bangladesh: Associations with Emotion, Social Expectations and Consumption.

Main question: How are viewing patterns for Indian, Turkish, Korean, Pakistani and other foreign serials associated with viewers’ emotional experiences, social expectations and consumption behavior?

  • Which languages, countries and genres do viewers watch, and why?
  • Which characters, family portrayals or relationship narratives attract viewers, and how do these compare with their own experiences?
  • Are viewing time and character identification associated with relationship expectations, aspirations, identity or consumer preferences?
  • How do these associations vary by age, gender, urban-rural location, education and platform?

Design: Begin with interviews and small focus groups with 30–40 viewers, followed by a stratified survey of approximately 600 adult viewers across several regions. Distinguish television serials from streaming series, and record country/language, viewing time, motivations and platform. Include online and offline viewers to reduce selection bias.

Measures and analysis: Use self-reported emotional responses, character identification, relationship expectations, identity, product awareness/preferences and time use. Use validated Bangla instruments where available; otherwise translate, back-translate and pilot. A survey can show associations; it cannot by itself show that serial viewing caused a change. Repeated surveys or diary methods would be needed to study change over time.

Ethics: Do not treat women viewers as a uniform group. Protect privacy and allow participants to skip questions about relationships, sexuality or mental health. Viewing preferences should not be framed as moral decline.

Shared research principles

All three proposals are preliminary. Each requires a fuller literature review, pilot testing, a justified sampling plan, analytic strategy, data protection and applicable ethics approval. Associations found in surveys should not be presented as causal effects.
