Marxist Vocabulary: Alienation and Alliance Politics

Some terms used to understand society and politics cannot be grasped by memorising a dictionary definition alone. We need to connect them to people’s work, the products of that work and relationships among people. This page explains two terms in plain language and relates them to Bangladesh. The local examples are illustrative; they are not reports verified at any particular factory or movement.

1. Alienation

In Marx’s writing, alienation describes a social relation in which people have limited control over their labour, its products, the work process and the development of their human capacities. Work and what it produces can confront workers as something external, subject to forces beyond their control. The term is not simply a name for feeling bored or dissatisfied at work; it also concerns material relations shaped by the ownership and organisation of production.

Consider a general example from Bangladesh’s garment industry. Workers make clothes for export, but may have little say over product design, the pace of production, working hours or safety decisions. Their products move through factory ownership and international markets; workers themselves do not decide how those products are used, priced or sold. This gap helps illustrate one dimension of alienation. Experiences differ by factory, task, bargaining power, union representation and management practices. Claims about a particular workplace therefore require evidence about workers’ experiences and working conditions.

The concept prompts questions: Who sets the pace and rules of work? How much can workers participate in decisions? Can they use their skills and creativity? Who controls the results of production, and how are those results distributed?

2. Alliance politics

Alliance politics means people or organisations with different identities, programmes and disagreements working together on a defined common demand while retaining their independence. Agreement on one issue does not mean agreement on everything. An alliance usually pursues a specific goal, with its participants cooperating without giving up their distinct organisations or perspectives. This is not a single foundational theory named by Marx; it is a term used to describe a strategy in left and social movements.

A hypothetical example in Bangladesh might bring garment-worker organisations, women’s rights advocates, students, public-health workers and environmental groups together to campaign for safe workplaces and fair wages. They would not need to adopt one ideology or long-term programme; they could agree on a few concrete demands. For such an alliance to work, workers’ voices and leadership need to remain central, decision-making should be clear, and stronger organisations should not drown out the demands of less powerful participants. This is a possible example, not a description of an existing coalition in Bangladesh.

Alliances also have limits. Winning a shared demand does not by itself change the wider structure of power and resources. An alliance must keep asking who makes decisions, whose interests receive priority and whether participants can retain their independence.

Two concepts, two different questions

Alienation helps us examine people’s control and position within work and production, and their relationship to what they produce. Alliance politics helps explain how people and organisations from different backgrounds can act together on a shared demand. Both can inform analysis of labour and social movements in Bangladesh: one raises questions about power and control at work, while the other concerns collective action for change.
