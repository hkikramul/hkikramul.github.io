Islam in Bengal: folk traditions, literature, and nineteenth-century reform

The religious history of Bengali Muslims is not a story of a single “local” or “foreign” current. Islamization, religious writing in Bangla, pir narratives, and reform movements all developed amid changing social and political conditions.

East Bengal Renaissance · Historical essay · November 24, 2026

A November 15, 2026 opinion column by Devdutt Pattanaik in The New Indian Express, “How Global Islam Erased Local Bengali Islam,” prompted the choice of this subject. This article is not a translation of that column. It is an independent historical account of Islam in Bengal, folk religious practice, and reform movements, based on historical scholarship. Read the original column.

The spread of Islam: neither sudden nor caused by one thing

The beginning of Muslim political rule in Bengal and the conversion of people in Bengal to Islam were separate historical processes. Turkish rulers established control over parts of Bengal in the early thirteenth century, but the growth of Muslim society across the delta took a long time and varied by region. Richard Eaton’s research considers political frontiers, the expansion of cultivated land, new settlements, and the development of village mosques and shrines together. He shows how the expansion of the agricultural frontier was connected to the growth of Islamic institutions and Muslim communities, particularly in eastern Bengal. [1]

This account does not reduce conversion to one cause. Rule, trade, Sufi networks, settlement, social relations, and personal belief operated differently across places and periods. “Islamization” refers not only to individual changes of religion but also to changes in institutions, practices, literature, and social identity. There is no historical basis for the idea that all people across Bengal became Muslim at the same time or for the same reason.

Religious knowledge and literature in local languages

Arabic and Persian had long-standing roles in Islamic scholarship. At the same time, Muslim poets and writers produced religious and literary works in Bangla. The sixteenth- and seventeenth-century poet Syed Sultan’s Nabibangsha presented the stories of the prophets in Bangla; puthi literature also made narratives such as Amir Hamza and Yusuf-Zulekha popular. Bangla literature created a space for religious ideas and imagination in a language familiar to local readers. [3, 4]

Calling this literature a “local translation” of a foreign religion does not capture all of its significance. Poets used familiar meters, narrative forms, and Bangla vocabulary to build their own religious and literary worlds. But writing in Bangla alone does not prove that every writer supported the same kind of religious synthesis.

Pirs, Satya Pir, and shared sacred spaces

In many parts of Bengal, devotion to pirs and shrines, vows, and practices around local sacred figures became part of Muslim religious life. Some Satya Pir narratives draw on the different religious vocabularies and storytelling forms of Hindu and Muslim audiences. Tony K. Stewart’s study of Satya Pir stories from the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries shows that the same figure cannot be reduced to one meaning across every text. [5]

These examples provide evidence of cultural exchange in Bengal’s religious life. They do not show that Hindu and Muslim beliefs became identical or that every village followed the same customs. Scholars also differ over what “syncretism” should mean in this context. It is important to make claims from evidence tied to specific stories, places, and periods.

Nineteenth-century reform: theology, colonial rule, and peasant life

Several Muslim reform and revival currents were active in nineteenth-century Bengal, including the Faraizi, Tariqa-i-Muhammadiya, Ahl-i-Hadith, and Tayuni movements. They differed over local practices, devotion to pirs, urs festivals, and customs such as Fatiha. Some reformers criticized certain practices as bid‘ah or later additions to the earliest teachings of Islam; scholars in other currents defended some inherited institutions. It is inaccurate to describe all reformers as a single group with one position. [2]

The Faraizi movement cannot be understood only as an effort to regulate religious practice. A religious reform initiative begun by Haji Shariatullah became, under Dudu Miyan, involved in broader struggles over peasants’ land, rents, and zamindari oppression. Nurul Hossein Choudhury’s study describes how the movement developed into a form of peasant resistance. Religious identity and economic claims became intertwined. [6]

These movements were also connected to Islamic thought that traveled across regions; Haji Shariatullah had studied in Mecca for many years. But local leadership, colonial rule, landlord relations, and the realities of rural society also shaped how reform took form in Bengal. It is incomplete to dismiss the movements as simply “pressure from outside Islam.” It is equally mistaken to treat reformers’ criticism of local customs as powerless or without effect.

Is “erasure” an accurate historical description?

Some reform currents questioned the legitimacy of particular pir traditions and local customs, creating tensions around religious authority and identity. But a sweeping claim that one organized campaign completely eliminated centuries of folk religious practice across all Bengal requires much broader evidence. Scholarship also records the persistence of pir devotion, puthi literature, and local customs in varied forms. Practices changed, disputes arose, and some customs weakened; the outcome was not the same everywhere.

History also becomes too simple if “global” and “local” Islam are treated as opposing camps. Religious ideas and reform movements crossed borders; people in Bengal received, changed, or rejected them through their own languages, social relations, and political experiences. This interaction is essential to understanding Islam in Bengal.

Conclusion

Local religious experience, Bangla literature, connections to the wider Muslim world, and nineteenth-century reform all belong to the history of Bengali Muslims. Making any one of them the sole force obscures the others. The most cautious historical conclusion is that Islam in Bengal is a diverse tradition that developed over many centuries and differently across regions; local forms and reform initiatives sometimes coexisted and sometimes came into conflict. This history cannot be reduced to “pure versus corrupted” or “local versus foreign.”
