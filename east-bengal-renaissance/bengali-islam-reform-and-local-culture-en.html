Folk Islam in Bengal: reform, culture, and contested identity

The religious lives of Bengali Muslims have developed in conversation with local languages, memories, and social experience. The history includes disputes over what counted as “pure” or “improper” practice, as well as the varied traditions of pirs, shrines, puthi literature, and village religious life.

East Bengal Renaissance · Bengali adaptation with context · November 24, 2026

About the source: This article is an adapted Bengali-language reading of Devdutt Pattanaik’s opinion column, “How Global Islam Erased Local Bengali Islam,” published in The New Indian Express on November 15, 2026. The argument has been reorganized for Bengali readers, with contextual notes and qualifications added to several historical claims. This is not a complete or line-by-line translation. Read the original column.

Religious life is more than the history of doctrine

Religious practice in a region cannot be understood only through scripture or formal instruction. Local languages, occupations, rivers and forests, illness and disaster, family customs, and faith in familiar sacred figures also shape everyday religion. In many parts of Bengal, pirs, shrines, vows, puthi texts, prayers for river journeys, and beliefs connected to forest life have been woven into Muslim religious experience. To dismiss them all as a “wrong version” of a religion is to disregard people’s lived history.

One important contribution of historian Asim Roy’s work is to consider Islam in Bengal not as an incomplete imitation of an unchanging Middle Eastern model, but as a historical religious tradition that developed over time in a particular region. Bengali Muslims wrote religious stories, poetry, and puthi literature in Bangla, using local literary forms and familiar imagery to explain Islamic ideas. [2]

Stories of Satya Pir: blending, or multiple interpretations?

Stories of Satya Pir are a familiar example of religious and cultural exchange in Bengal. In some narratives and devotional practices he appears as a Muslim pir; elsewhere he is linked with Satyanarayan or Krishna, or situated at the meeting point of two traditions. Tony K. Stewart’s research brings to light the varied literary forms found in Bengali manuscripts and stories about Satya Pir. [4]

However, the fact that Hindus and Muslims may make vows to or honor the same pir does not mean that everyone held identical beliefs. Different communities can understand a sacred figure or place in different ways. So even when using terms such as “blending” or “syncretism,” we should avoid presenting local belief as one uniform, fixed system. Practices changed across region, time, class, and family.

Nineteenth-century reform: more than a campaign for “foreign purity”

Several Muslim reform currents, including the Faraizi, Tariqa-i-Muhammadiya, and Ahl-i-Hadith movements, were active in nineteenth-century Bengal. Some criticized pir shrines, urs festivals, or local customs as bid‘ah or religious accretions; other currents defended some inherited institutions. It is therefore inaccurate to assume all “reformers” said the same thing or acted in the same way. [3]

The history of the Faraizi movement shows why religious reform cannot be separated from the economic conditions of peasants. An initiative begun by Haji Shariatullah later became, under Dudu Miyan, entangled with wider conflicts over zamindari oppression, rents, and peasant rights. Researcher Nurul Hossein Choudhury describes its development from religious reform into an agrarian movement. Religious language served not only as a language of belief but also of social solidarity and protest. [5]

Explaining criticism of local custom only as “pressure from foreign Islam” obscures local leadership, colonial rule, landlord relations, and rural class conflict. At the same time, describing reform as a neutral effort to purify religion can hide its effects on local memory and culture. Both belong in the historical account.

The limits of the word “erasure”

The original column’s headline speaks of local Bengali Islam being “erased.” It is a forceful interpretive headline, but it should not be taken as proof that complete disappearance occurred in the same way everywhere. The column itself notes that pir devotion and puthi literature persisted in many places. Practices changed, some customs faced challenge, and others continued with new meanings. “Erasure” can serve as a metaphor for experiences of power and cultural loss; it is not necessarily a literal, universal description of history.

Islam in Bengal cannot be divided into something “only local” or “only imported.” Religious ideas and reform currents traveled across regions, while people in Bengal received or rejected them through local languages, social realities, and debates. Local experience and connection to the wider Muslim world existed side by side.

Conclusion

The central lesson is that judging a community’s religious life by a single standard of “purity” can erase many voices from history. Bengal’s pir traditions, puthi literature, reform movements, and peasant protests are not substitutes for one another; they are histories formed where religion, language, power, and livelihood meet. We can recognize the importance of local traditions without claiming that everyone shared one blended belief. And we cannot write the history of religious reform by erasing the experiences of local people.
