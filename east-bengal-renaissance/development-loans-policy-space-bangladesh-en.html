Development Loans, Policy Space and Bangladesh’s AI Proposal

Loans from the Asian Development Bank and the World Bank can help countries build infrastructure and public services. Yet when foreign-currency exposure, policy conditions, weak implementation, and social costs are poorly managed, the same financing can erode a country’s capacity to develop.

The central argument is that development loans cannot be judged by whether money was disbursed or a project was completed. We must ask who set the conditions, in what currency and at what cost the debt will be repaid, who receives the benefits, and who bears the burden if the project fails or the economy is hit by a shock. This is not an argument against borrowing or technology. It is an argument for democratic control, financial sustainability, and fair distribution.

1. Development finance is not one kind of loan

Project loans usually finance a defined asset or service. They may include procurement rules, environmental and social safeguards, audits, reporting, and implementation requirements. Policy-based loans provide budget support or finance a reform programme; under ADB policy, disbursement can depend on completion of agreed policy actions. The World Bank’s Development Policy Financing likewise involves prior actions and assessment of the macroeconomic framework. Technical assistance, project loans, and policy-based loans should not be treated as interchangeable: their conditions and risks differ. [1–3]

Interest, maturity, grace periods, and fees also vary across ADB and World Bank products. Some financing is highly concessional and long-term; other lending can move with market rates. It is therefore inaccurate to say that every development loan is exploitative—or that a loan is risk-free simply because it comes from a multilateral development bank. The relevant evidence is the specific agreement: its currency, charges, conditions, and risk assessment. [4]

2. Foreign-currency debt: domestic benefits, external-currency obligations

A loan denominated in foreign currency must be repaid in foreign currency. Yet the benefit of a road, hospital, agricultural extension programme, or digital public service may appear in better living standards and productivity rather than in direct dollar earnings. If the local currency depreciates, exports fall, or import costs rise, the government needs more domestic resources to service the same debt. This does not prove that a project is unwise; it means that social returns must be assessed alongside currency and repayment risks.

In a country with a narrow tax base and limited foreign-exchange buffers, a rising debt-service bill may leave governments choosing among new taxes, spending cuts, or additional borrowing. These choices are not distributionally neutral. Cuts to social protection, primary health care, education, agricultural support, or local infrastructure can weigh most heavily on low-income households and workers. Debt risk is therefore not only a matter for the finance ministry; it is also a question of distribution and power.

3. Conditionality, policy autonomy, and democratic legitimacy

Lenders often justify policy conditions as safeguards for public money, macroeconomic stability, or reform implementation. Those goals can be legitimate. But when reforms are agreed without adequate deliberation among parliament, workers, farmers, small businesses, users of public services, and civil society, a government’s room to set policy may narrow. “Country ownership” can exist on paper while dependence on urgent financing shifts bargaining power toward the lender and executive agencies.

The number or label of conditions does not by itself show whether they are fair. The substantive questions are whether a condition is directly connected to the loan’s purpose, whether public agencies can implement it, whether affected groups were consulted, whether essential services could lose financing if it is missed, and whether alternative policies were considered. ADB’s own independent evaluation has identified risks from complex designs, numerous conditions, weak implementation capacity, and inadequate accounting for direct and indirect costs. [5]

Much of the most critical research on conditionality focuses on the IMF. One study analysed 55,465 policy conditions in IMF agreements across 131 countries from 1985 to 2014, raising questions about policy space and the integration of social protections. Another study examined poverty and particular structural conditions in 81 developing countries. These findings cannot be attributed to every ADB or World Bank loan. Institutions, instruments, and country contexts differ. The research does, however, identify a testable risk: the social cost of crisis-era reforms must be measured and addressed in advance. [6, 7]

4. If a project succeeds, who benefits?

Physical completion is not, by itself, development success. Who gains access? Who pays? Did the project build domestic expertise and maintenance capacity, or does it leave the country dependent on foreign suppliers for technology, spare parts, and servicing? Will extra debt and operating costs squeeze other public services? A project may contribute to measured growth without strengthening the economy’s resilience if benefits accrue to a narrow group while costs are spread across the public.

Large project loans can involve major contracts, consultancies, procurement, land decisions, and permits. In agencies with weak capacity, these processes can create risks of cost escalation, poor contract oversight, biased selection, or political patronage. This does not establish corruption in every project. It explains why open tenders, published contracts, independent audits, and accessible grievance mechanisms matter.

5. Bangladesh: debt risk alongside urgent public needs

The IMF’s 2026 debt assessment put Bangladesh’s public and publicly guaranteed debt at about 41 percent of GDP in FY25. It classified the risk of debt distress as “moderate,” while noting limited room to absorb shocks. “Moderate risk” does not mean that Bangladesh is insolvent or unable to repay. It does mean that new borrowing requires careful analysis of costs, foreign-exchange exposure, revenues, and plausible shocks. [8]

The World Bank estimated that Bangladesh’s national poverty rate rose from 18.7 percent in 2022 to 21.4 percent in 2025. Households also face substantial out-of-pocket health costs, while safe water, reliable electricity and gas, farm inputs, rent, and learning outcomes remain pressing concerns. In this setting, a proposed technology investment should be compared with alternatives: what would the same financing do for agricultural productivity, primary care, school learning, safe water, or reliable energy? [9]

6. Bangladesh’s “Bangla AI” proposal: known facts and unknown terms

A 11 November 2026 report described an indicative $250 million programme under discussion with ADB. It includes Bangla-language AI as well as cybersecurity and technology villages. The report said the financing structure and implementation schedule had not been finalised. The figure should therefore not be described as an approved loan, a loan with final terms, or money earmarked solely for a Bangla AI model. A separate ADB digital-economy project page describes proposed technical assistance to prepare diagnostics and future investments. [10, 11]

Some current multilingual models support Bengali, and Bangla-specific models and research already exist. But language support does not guarantee accuracy across Bangladesh’s dialects, legal and administrative context, or high-stakes uses in health and education. A case for a new national model should answer: why is it better than adapting existing models? Which public problem will it solve? Who benefits? How will privacy and data rights be protected? Who is accountable when it makes a consequential error? [12]

“Technological sovereignty” and keeping pace with the world can be legitimate policy aims. They do not, by themselves, establish public value. Compelling narratives around large technology projects must not obscure total costs, alternatives, beneficiaries, procurement, maintenance, and accountability. Government white papers, investigations, and news reporting have raised concerns about irregularities, overspending, and weak implementation in earlier ICT projects. These reports do not prove corruption in the current proposal; they strengthen the case for transparency and independent audit. [13]

7. A social-democratic standard for development finance

From a social-democratic perspective, development is not simply GDP growth, new infrastructure, or technological prestige. It also asks who receives the gains from production, whether workers gain bargaining power, whether farmers have fair access to inputs and markets, and whether health care and education are genuinely accessible. Strategic public investment, strong public services, progressive revenue, labour rights, cooperative enterprise, and democratic accountability belong in the same discussion. The work of Ha-Joon Chang and Robert Wade on industrialisation shows why development strategies must account for historical conditions and state capacity rather than assume that one market formula suits every country. [14, 15]

The question, then, is not simply “Should the country borrow?” or “Should it adopt AI?” It is whether the loan serves the public, whether its conditions receive democratic scrutiny, whether risk is fairly shared, and whether the country becomes more capable after the project ends. Calling debt-financed technology indispensable while basic needs remain unmet is a narrow definition of development.

What should be disclosed before a loan is approved?

  • The loan currency, interest or reference rate, maturity, grace period, every fee, and repayment schedule.
  • Separate accounts for project lending, policy-based lending, grants, and technical assistance.
  • All policy actions linked to disbursement, their legal basis, and their expected public effects.
  • Feasibility studies, alternatives, lifecycle costs, maintenance plans, and comparison with existing technologies.
  • Tender documents, contracts, subcontractors, beneficiaries, and independent audit findings.
  • Public scenarios showing repayment pressure under currency depreciation, lower export earnings, cost overruns, or disasters.
  • Open consultations with workers, farmers, teachers, health workers, technical experts, civil society, and affected communities.

Conclusion

ADB and World Bank finance can support essential infrastructure, public services, and institutional capacity. Poor bargaining, currency mismatch, badly designed conditions, weak institutions, or neglect of social costs can also reduce a country’s future capacity. Each loan should therefore be judged on its own contract, alternatives, evidence, and outcomes. Criticism should target policies and documented results—not declare individuals or institutions guilty without evidence.

Measure development not by a project’s branding or technological scale, but by whether people’s lives become more affordable, secure, democratic, and dignified.
