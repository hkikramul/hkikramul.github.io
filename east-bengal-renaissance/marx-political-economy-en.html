Karl Marx’s Political Economy and Bangladesh’s Garment Industry

In a garment factory, materials, machinery and human work come together to produce shirts for export. Karl Marx’s political economy asks how ownership and wage relations organise production and who controls its surplus. This essay explains his framework using Bangladesh’s ready-made garment industry as a setting.

1. Value, labour and labour-power

For Marx, the value of a commodity is governed by socially necessary labour time, the time required to produce it under prevailing conditions with average skill and intensity. A slower worker does not thereby create a more valuable shirt. Market price and theoretical value are distinct concepts. [1, 4]

Labour-power is the capacity to work. Labour is the exercise of that capacity. A wage contract gives the employer the use of labour-power for a specified period. Marx relates the value of labour-power to the means of subsistence required to maintain workers and reproduce the workforce. These requirements have a historical and social dimension. Actual wages can differ from that theoretical value. [1]

2. Surplus value and its rate

In Marx’s simplified model, total commodity value W has three components. c is constant capital transferred to the product, including fabric, thread and the portion of machinery used up. v is variable capital advanced to purchase labour-power. s is surplus value.

W = c + v + s
New value created = v + s

During necessary labour time (tn), workers create new value equivalent to the value of their labour-power. The remainder is surplus labour time (ts). Assuming labour-power is purchased at its value and equal amounts of new value are added per hour, the following relationship holds. [1, 2]

Rate of surplus value = (s / v) × 100% = (ts / tn) × 100%

Suppose a hypothetical batch has a total value of Tk 10,000, transferred value of Tk 6,000 and wages of Tk 2,500. Surplus value is Tk 1,500, and the rate is 1,500 ÷ 2,500 × 100% = 60%. The 60% expresses surplus value relative to wages. The earlier example used Tk 10,000 as sales revenue. Here it stands for theoretical total value to illustrate the calculation. Observed sales and costs cannot directly identify Marxian surplus value or its rate. [2, 4]

3. The working day, technology and exploitation

Extending the working day while holding necessary labour time fixed produces what Marx calls absolute surplus value. With the working day held fixed, a fall in the value of labour-power shortens necessary labour time and increases relative surplus value. This can occur if widespread productivity gains reduce the value of goods required to sustain workers and their households. Higher output after a machinery upgrade at one garment factory does not by itself establish such a change. [3]

In Marx’s framework, exploitation describes the appropriation of surplus labour through the wage relation. It can occur even when the employer pays the agreed wage on time. Wage theft and breaches of labour law are separate questions that require their own evidence. The labour theory of value and its relationship to market prices remain subjects of scholarly debate. [2, 4]

4. What can be studied in Bangladesh’s garment industry?

Wages, hours and productivity within factories matter, as do the prices, lead times and changes in order volume set by international buyers. Mark Anner examines how buyer purchasing practices relate to working conditions and labour rights at supplier factories in Bangladesh. Better Work Bangladesh reports on pay, safety and social dialogue in participating factories. Those results cannot be generalised to the entire industry without further evidence. [5, 6]

One testable question is how changes in productivity or buyer prices relate to real hourly pay, overtime and work intensity across comparable factories. Factory production and payroll records, combined with worker interviews, could help answer it. Marx’s framework directs attention to how the gains from production are distributed across the supply chain.
