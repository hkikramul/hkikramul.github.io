History · Religion & society · Marriage

Marriage in Arabia: What preceded Islam, and what changed after the prophetic mission?

The short answer: There was no single marriage custom shared by all of pre-Islamic Arabia. Practices varied by community, region, status, and time. After the prophetic mission began, the Qur’an and the early Muslim community retained marriage as a familiar social institution while articulating new rules and limits concerning consent, dower, and women’s rights. Islam did not “invent marriage”; nor is it historically sound to describe every pre-Islamic Arabian marriage as one uniform practice.

Pre-Islamic Arabia: multiple practices, uneven evidence

What we know about pre-Islamic Arabia comes through sources such as hadith compiled later, sira literature, poetry, and other historical accounts. These sources are valuable, but they are not modern contemporaneous censuses or neutral field surveys. A careful account therefore avoids turning one report into a claim about what “all Arabs” did.

In a report in Sahih al-Bukhari, Aisha describes four forms of relationship or marriage said to have existed before Islam. One was a family-arranged marriage. Another account describes a husband permitting his wife to have relations with a man chosen to seek particular lineage; a third involves several men and later identification of a child’s paternity; and a fourth is a form of public sex work. The report says that Muhammad retained the form recognized as marriage and abolished the others. [1]

It is more cautious to read this report as a memory and moral assessment of reform in early Muslim tradition, not as a complete survey of every pre-Islamic Arabian community. Modern scholarship likewise calls for a more varied account of marriage contracts and their application across the Jahiliyya and early Islamic periods. [6, 7]

What became clearer after the prophetic mission?

Dower as a woman’s right: Qur’an 4:4 instructs believers to give women their dower willingly. This supports understanding the dower as the wife’s own financial right, rather than simply a payment between families or a “price” for a bride. [2]

Against coercion and control through inheritance: Qur’an 4:19 prohibits taking women as “inheritance” against their will and warns against trying to reclaim what was given to them unjustly. [3]

Consent in marriage: A report in Sahih Muslim addresses the consent of previously married and virgin women: the previously married woman has greater authority over her own decision, while a virgin’s permission must be sought. This hadith must be read alongside Qur’anic interpretation, jurisprudence, and differences among legal schools. By itself, it does not prove that consent was always secured in social practice. [4]

Limits and justice in polygyny: Qur’an 4:3 permits marriage to more than one wife under specified conditions, while directing a man who fears he cannot act justly to marry one. The verse’s historical setting and later legal interpretations merit separate study; summarizing it as a bare “permission” leaves out its condition of justice. [5]

When did Muhammad and Khadija marry?

According to the sira tradition, Muhammad and Khadija married before his prophetic mission—that is, before the first revelation. Their marriage should therefore not be presented as an example of rules developed after the mission began. The account reaches us through later biographical literature, and historians have debated some details, including ages, dates, and particular events. [8]

What can history tell us?

The changes associated with the prophetic mission are not best described as the “invention” of marriage. They are better understood as a process of placing a familiar social institution within a religious and legal framework: prohibiting certain practices, identifying the dower as the wife’s right, addressing coercion, and articulating responsibilities and justice within family life. Textual rules and their actual application in society are not the same thing. Historical writing should keep those questions distinct, acknowledge the limits of its sources, and resist reducing the past to a present-day slogan.
