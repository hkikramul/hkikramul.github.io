Artificial Intelligence: Progress, Power and the Human Future

Artificial intelligence promises to make many tasks easier. It also raises questions about ownership, labor, environmental costs, and control over human knowledge.

An edited adaptation of ideas from Arundhati Roy’s “Apocalypse Now and Then,” prepared for East Bengal Renaissance readers. This is not a full translation of the original essay. Read the original essay at EQUATOR.

Artificial intelligence presents us with two pictures at once. It can help translate languages, support medical work, make information easier to find, and assist creative projects. It can also expand surveillance, unsettle jobs, concentrate control over information, and place new demands on the environment. Arundhati Roy’s central question is whether we are paying attention only to what AI can do, or also to what it is doing to people and society.

Power is at the center of Roy’s concern. A small number of large technology companies use vast collections of writing, images, art, research, and everyday data to build AI systems. Yet important questions often remain unanswered: who supplied this material, was it used with permission, and who benefits from the value created from it? If knowledge produced collectively becomes the property of a few companies, control over knowledge may become concentrated as well.

These questions concern more than writers and artists. AI systems require computing capacity, electricity, water, and land. Data centers are built in particular places, while their benefits and profits may not be shared equally with nearby communities. The labor involved in sorting data, reviewing model outputs, and preparing systems for use can also remain largely invisible. Any honest account of AI must include these human and environmental costs.

Roy also draws attention to military applications and state surveillance. A technology that can assist with translation or medicine can also be used to track people, extend monitoring, or identify targets in war. The consequences depend on who controls the system, what it is used for, and who is held accountable when it causes harm.

The debate contains both dramatic predictions of disaster and claims that technology can solve nearly every problem. Roy’s essay is most useful when it moves beyond those extremes and asks who owns AI, whose labor supports it, who pays its costs, and how the public can hold powerful institutions accountable. Her arguments should be read as those of a political essay, not as proof that every risk she describes is already established. Individual factual claims still need to be assessed on their evidence.

Questions for East Bengal

For East Bengal and other regions with fewer resources, these are practical questions. Will knowledge produced in Bengali have a meaningful place in future technologies? Who will set the rules for using our writing, images, and language data? How might the electricity and water demands of data centers affect local communities? And will AI’s benefits reach students, teachers, workers, and patients—or mainly those who own the systems?

Neither uncritical adoption nor total rejection is an adequate response. What is needed is transparency, fair rules for data use, protection for workers, environmental accountability, and independent oversight. Whether AI supports human knowledge and creativity or turns that shared inheritance into private property is a decision that cannot be left to technology companies alone.
