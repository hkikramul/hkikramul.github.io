Labour and economics · Public policy · Workers’ rights

Bangladesh’s Labour Policy, Labour Law and Reform Commission

East Bengal Renaissance · 13 November 2026

Summary: The Ministry of Labour and Employment’s policy list still includes the Bangladesh Labour Policy 2012. It separately lists the National Employment Policy 2022, Domestic Workers Protection and Welfare Policy 2015, and National Occupational Health and Safety Policy 2013. The 2025 Labour Reform Commission made wide-ranging recommendations on worker recognition, wages, organizing, safety and social protection. A policy or commission recommendation does not become law by itself; each must be checked against legislation and implementation records.

1. What does “labour policy” mean?

A labour policy sets the state’s goals and priorities: what kinds of employment, wages, working conditions and employer-worker relations it seeks to promote. Labour legislation turns rights and duties into binding rules, while regulations, gazettes and court decisions set out how they are applied. A right or objective stated in a policy is not automatically an enforceable legal entitlement for every worker. The law’s coverage, definitions, exclusions and enforcement matter.

2. Bangladesh Labour Policy 2012: goals and limits

The policy’s stated aims include building a skilled workforce, expanding employment, protecting workers in domestic and international labour markets, promoting decent and safe working conditions, strengthening social protection, and addressing the rights of women, persons with disabilities, marginalized communities and children at risk of hazardous work. It refers to workers in both formal and informal sectors. It also identifies tripartite dialogue among government, workers and employers as an important basis for labour policy.

These are broad policy commitments. They require legislation, budgets, inspection, freedom for workers to organize, safe complaint channels and regular data to become practical protections. The policy’s age also matters: the economy and labour market of 2012 differ from today’s digital and platform work, climate risks and supply-chain arrangements. Reviewing the policy and publishing implementation progress would be reasonable steps.

3. Related policies should be read together

  • National Employment Policy 2022: a policy framework for job creation, skills, productivity and an inclusive labour market.
  • Domestic Workers Protection and Welfare Policy 2015: a policy commitment concerning domestic workers’ dignity, safety, working conditions and complaints. It is not a substitute for a complete statutory labour framework.
  • National Occupational Health and Safety Policy 2013: guidance on workplace health risks, prevention, safety measures and institutional responsibilities.

The Ministry lists these as separate documents. They need to be read alongside labour legislation, rules and sector-specific gazettes. A policy may promise protection while the available legal remedy still depends on a worker’s legal status, the kind of workplace and the applicable statutory provision.

4. Labour law: working hours and minimum wages

Under the current text of the Bangladesh Labour Act 2006, the ordinary working time for an adult worker covered by the Act is eight hours a day and 48 hours a week. Subject to the Act’s overtime provisions, daily work may extend to ten hours; total time may not exceed 60 hours in a week, with an annual average not above 56 hours a week. The Act provides for additional overtime pay. These figures should not be assumed to apply automatically to every occupation and worker: statutory coverage, exclusions, type of work and later amendments must be checked.

For minimum wages, sections 139–140 allow the government to ask a wage board to recommend rates for an industry or class of workers, which the government may declare by gazette. A 2025 amendment shortened the scheduled review interval from five years to three. This is a sector/class-based mechanism; it is not the same as the universal national wage floor proposed by the Commission. A gazetted sectoral minimum is binding on the relevant employers.

The Bangladesh Labour (Amendment) Act 2026 made further changes to labour legislation. Its enactment does not, by itself, mean that every Commission recommendation has been adopted. Each proposal needs to be compared with the enacted sections and gazettes.

5. The Labour Reform Commission report (April 2025)

The government-appointed Labour Reform Commission issued its report in April 2025 under the title “A Roadmap for the Transformation of the World of Work: Workers’ Rights, Well-Coordinated Industrial Relations and Inclusive Development.” The full report is 458 pages. It discusses workers, employers, public agencies, civil society and different groups of workers. It is a set of policy recommendations; whether a particular proposal became enforceable law must be established separately from subsequent legislation, gazettes and government decisions.

The report is not limited to the garment industry. It considers agriculture, informal work, domestic work, self-employment and migrant workers, among others. Its themes include legal recognition, wages, safety, social protection, organizing and collective bargaining, dispute resolution, discrimination, child labour and forced labour.

Selected Commission recommendations

  • A national minimum wage basis: establish a national wage floor informed by living costs and a dignified standard of life, with sectoral rates not below that floor. It also proposes regular adjustment for inflation and costs and review every three years.
  • A standing wage institution: create a permanent national wage commission and sectoral committees involving worker and employer representatives and labour expertise. Representation should be transparent and accountable.
  • Recognition and legal protection: extend meaningful rights and remedies to workers outside conventional employment structures, including informal, agricultural, self-employed and migrant workers.
  • Organizing and collective bargaining: make workers’ paths to organizing, representation and bargaining simpler, participatory and accountable.
  • Safety and compensation: strengthen accident prevention, occupational health, effective inspection, compensation for workplace injuries and worker complaint processes.
  • Equality and social protection: expand anti-discrimination protections and social security while accounting for the experiences of women and gender-diverse workers, persons with disabilities, young and older workers, ethnic minorities and migrant workers.

6. Recommendations are different from enacted law

The report is significant because it treats labour policy as a question for the wider workforce, not only wage bargaining in factories. But its recommendations must not be quoted as the current legal position. For example, the Commission proposes a national wage floor and permanent wage commission; the present statutory framework provides for sectoral rates through wage boards. Closing that gap may require new legislation, regulations, budgets and administrative institutions.

The 2026 labour amendment includes new or revised provisions concerning safety, workplace injuries, organizing and complaint remedies. These are meaningful developments, but the extent to which each Commission proposal has been adopted should not be assumed without a clause-by-clause public comparison. Implementation also requires regulations, inspectors, funds, complaint resolution and accessible information for workers.

7. Social-democratic priorities for implementation

An inclusive labour system need not treat workers’ dignity, productivity and the sustainable capacity of firms as competing goals. Several steps would help turn policy commitments into practice:

  1. Update national labour policy: assess implementation of the 2012 policy and publish a new plan with measurable goals, responsible agencies, budgets and timelines.
  2. Use open evidence in wage-setting: consider living costs, productivity, regional differences and firm capacity using public data; define a phased path that protects workers at the bottom of the wage distribution.
  3. Make tripartite representation transparent: publish how government, worker and employer representatives are selected and how women, informal and migrant workers can be heard. Participation should include clear selection rules, conflict disclosures, minutes and records of disagreement.
  4. Provide effective remedies: create safe, confidential and accessible complaint routes, independent investigation, protection from retaliation, timely decisions and a right of appeal.
  5. Fit social protection to the work: expand portable access to registration, health and injury protection, maternity benefits and old-age support for informal and self-employed workers.
  6. Publish implementation data: report inspections, injuries, unpaid wages, complaint outcomes and organizing rights regularly, with privacy safeguards and appropriate breakdowns by sector, region and gender.

Protecting informal workers takes more than registration or penalties. It requires simple rules suited to how work is organized, access to social protection and a safe way to seek remedies. Sustainable businesses and employment are also part of labour policy: public cost data, productivity improvements, fair purchasing contracts and worker representation should be considered together.

Conclusion

It is inaccurate to say Bangladesh has no labour policy. The country has the 2012 national policy, later employment and issue-specific policies, labour legislation and the Labour Reform Commission’s 2025 roadmap. The central questions are how these policies fit together, how many workers receive effective legal protection, and whether implementation responsibilities and results are public. Making workers’ rights real requires a sustained link among policy commitments, legal guarantees, organized worker voice, administrative capacity and accountability.
