Bangladesh’s International Crimes Tribunal: History and Controversy from an International Perspective

The Tribunal grew from a long demand for accountability for 1971 atrocities. Its record also raises enduring questions about fair trials, political independence, retroactive lawmaking, selectivity, and how a society can pursue justice without turning it into vengeance.

Evidence-informed historical explainer · Updated 12 November 2026

Terminology. Bangladesh’s International Crimes Tribunal (ICT) is a domestic court created under the International Crimes (Tribunals) Act, 1973. It is not the International Criminal Court (ICC), a United Nations tribunal, or an international court seated in The Hague. “International” describes the kinds of crimes within its jurisdiction; the Tribunal is part of Bangladesh’s national legal system. [1]

1. Why the Tribunal was created

The 1971 Liberation War involved grave violence against civilians, including mass killings, sexual violence, forced displacement, and persecution. Bangladesh’s early state-building included efforts to prosecute alleged collaborators. In 1972, the Collaborators Order enabled domestic proceedings; the 1973 Act established a legal framework for prosecuting genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes, and other crimes under international law. Parliament enacted the Act on 20 July 1973. [1][4]

The post-war justice process did not follow a straight line. A 1973 amnesty ended many collaborator cases, while serious offences such as murder, rape, and arson were excluded from that amnesty. In 1974, Bangladesh, India, and Pakistan reached an agreement on repatriation and other post-war issues. Bangladesh decided, as an act of clemency, not to proceed against 195 Pakistani prisoners of war then held in India; they were returned to Pakistan. This left a major gap between the Act’s broad language and the defendants ultimately available for prosecution. [3][4]

After the 1975 assassination of Sheikh Mujibur Rahman and subsequent political changes, the 1973 framework remained largely dormant. Civil-society campaigns for accountability revived in later decades, including a People’s Tribunal in 1992. The Awami League returned to office in 2009 after campaigning on war-crimes trials; the government reactivated the Act through amendments and established the first Tribunal in March 2010. [4][5]

2. A brief timeline

  • 1972–1973: Domestic collaborator proceedings begin; Parliament enacts the International Crimes (Tribunals) Act.
  • 1974: Bangladesh, India, and Pakistan settle major repatriation questions; Bangladesh does not proceed against 195 Pakistani POWs as an act of clemency.
  • 1990s–2000s: Civil-society campaigns keep demands for trials alive.
  • 2009–2010: The government revives the Act, makes amendments, and establishes the Tribunal.
  • 2013: The first major verdicts ignite the Shahbagh movement, counter-mobilization by Hefazat-e-Islam, and the Shapla Chattar crisis.
  • 2026: Parliament amends the Act again; the Tribunal is also handling cases concerning alleged crimes after 2009, including the Shapla Chattar case.

3. What the international debate has been about

International organisations did not generally argue that Bangladesh should abandon accountability. Human Rights Watch (HRW), the International Commission of Jurists (ICJ), and the International Center for Transitional Justice (ICTJ) acknowledged the victims’ claim to justice while warning that the process had to satisfy fair-trial standards. The distinction matters: scrutiny of a court’s procedure is not denial of the underlying atrocities, and support for accountability does not require endorsing every verdict or sentence. [5][6][7]

Fair-trial guarantees and independence

Critics raised concerns about the original Act and its early procedures: whether the defence had adequate time and facilities, could question witnesses effectively, could access independent expert testimony, and could challenge important procedural decisions. Observers also questioned whether political pressure, changes in judicial panels, or public demands for particular punishments could compromise confidence in judicial independence. HRW documented concerns about witness protection and alleged interference in particular cases; the prosecution and Tribunal disputed some of those accounts. These are documented controversies, not a basis for assuming that every defendant was innocent or every conviction invalid. [5][8]

Another concern was the Tribunal’s use of contempt powers against journalists and critics. Contempt powers can protect the administration of justice, but broad or unpredictable use can chill reporting and public discussion, especially in a politically charged trial. The international concern was that a court tasked with historic accountability should be open to reasoned scrutiny as well as protected from intimidation. [8]

Retroactivity and the rule of law

There are two different retroactivity debates. First, the 1973 Act was enacted after the 1971 crimes. Defenders of the trials point out that genocide, war crimes, and crimes against humanity were already recognized in international law; the legal question is how those rules were sufficiently defined and foreseeable at the time, and how they were applied to each accused. Scholars have debated the Tribunal’s relationship to evolving international criminal law. [4][9]

Second, in 2013 Parliament amended section 21 so the government or complainant could appeal an acquittal or sentence. The change was made retroactive to 14 July 2009. It followed the life sentence imposed on Abdul Quader Mollah and the Shahbagh demand for a death sentence. After the amendment, the prosecution appealed and the Supreme Court’s Appellate Division imposed the death penalty. HRW and ICTJ argued that changing appeal rights after a verdict and applying the change to Mollah’s case undermined fair-trial guarantees and the principle of legality. These were serious international-law criticisms; they should be identified as critics’ assessments rather than presented as a judgment by an international court. [6][10]

Bangladesh is party to the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR). Articles 14 and 15 protect fair-hearing rights and the principle that criminal liability and punishment must have a legal basis. Article 14(7) also addresses repeated trials or punishment after a final judgment. How these provisions apply to a particular appellate procedure is a legal question; the 2013 amendment became controversial precisely because it altered the rules after the original sentence. [11]

Selection of defendants and the problem of incomplete accountability

Most defendants in the Tribunal’s widely reported cases were Bangladeshi political or social figures accused of collaboration. Pakistani military officers and government officials who were not brought before the Tribunal remained outside its practical case docket. HRW and other observers argued that the absence of senior Pakistani suspects narrowed accountability and made the process look selective. Supporters responded that the inability to try every alleged perpetrator should not erase the possibility of prosecuting people within the court’s reach. Both points can be true: prosecutions can deliver important justice while leaving major questions unanswered. [5]

4. Shahbagh and Shapla Chattar: two mobilisations, one polarised moment

On 5 February 2013, the Tribunal convicted Abdul Quader Mollah on several counts and sentenced him to life imprisonment. Large demonstrations began at Shahbagh demanding the death penalty. For participants, Shahbagh represented a popular demand that the 1971 atrocities not be met with impunity and that political actors associated with collaboration be held accountable. The movement also came to express secular and nationalist ideas about Bangladesh’s identity. Critics warned that a mass demand for execution could pressure the courts and that the movement became entangled with party politics. Shahbagh was broad and internally diverse; it should not be reduced either to a government campaign or to a single, politically neutral citizens’ movement. [10][12]

In May 2013, Hefazat-e-Islam mobilised in Dhaka around a 13-point platform that included demands for punishment of bloggers it considered blasphemous and for stronger protection of Islamic values. The organisation and its supporters framed their mobilisation as a defence of religion and social morality. Opponents viewed parts of the platform as a threat to secularism, gender equality, and freedom of expression. These competing positions help explain the political context; they do not settle what happened during the security operation.

On the night of 5–6 May, security forces cleared the Shapla Chattar gathering. The government’s account disputed claims of deaths during the night operation, while rights groups and other sources reported deaths and injuries. HRW interviewed journalists and witnesses and documented the use of force and the evacuation of the area; it also said that some very high casualty claims circulating at the time were not supported by the evidence it could verify. Odhikar reported 61 deaths. The exact toll and responsibility require careful case-by-case evidence; online lists and partisan statements should not be treated as judicial findings. [13][14]

5. The Tribunal’s changing role: the 2026 amendments

The International Crimes (Tribunals) (Amendment) Act, 2026 updated the 1973 Act. Among its changes, it more explicitly set out accused persons’ rights to counsel, adequate preparation time, cross-examination, interpretation, and trial without undue delay. It also directed the Tribunal to refer to definitions and Elements of Crimes in the Rome Statute where they do not conflict with the domestic Act. The amendment’s commencement is stated as 6 January 2009. [2]

Those provisions respond to several longstanding concerns. At the same time, the Act now operates in proceedings about events well after 1971. The retroactive commencement date, changes to definitions and modes of liability, and the treatment of organisations raise questions that should be answered through transparent statutory interpretation and adversarial judicial review. It is essential to distinguish a procedural reform from a retroactive expansion of criminal liability; whether any specific charge is lawful depends on the exact provision, the date and conduct alleged, and the evidence.

That distinction is relevant to the Shapla Chattar case. In July 2026, the Tribunal took cognisance of formal charges against 41 people in a case concerning the 2013 events. The prosecution alleges that 58 people were killed across several districts. As of 12 November 2026, proceedings were still at the charge-framing stage; no merits judgment in this case had established the guilt of any accused. A prosecutor’s allegation, the Tribunal’s acceptance of a charge for proceedings, and a final conviction are different legal stages. [15][16]

6. An international-standard assessment

The Tribunal is best understood as a domestic accountability mechanism operating in the shadow of mass atrocity, delayed justice, and intense political conflict. Its strongest claim to legitimacy is that it gives victims and survivors a national forum to seek truth and accountability for crimes that had long gone untried. Its greatest risks are political selectivity, weak procedural safeguards, punishment-driven public pressure, and the use of a special court in ways that could appear to serve the government of the day.

International criminal law does not require a domestic tribunal to copy the ICC or the former Yugoslavia and Rwanda tribunals. It does require legality, individualised proof, independent judges, equality of arms, protection of witnesses, reasoned decisions, meaningful appeal, and no use of criminal process as collective or partisan punishment. Capital punishment adds a further concern because an execution cannot be reversed if a conviction is later shown to be unsafe.

A defensible approach therefore holds two commitments together: victims of 1971 and of later alleged abuses have a right to truth and justice; every accused person has a right to a fair and independent trial. Neither commitment cancels the other. The Shahbagh and Shapla narratives also should not be used as substitutes for evidence: the identity, political affiliation, or public reputation of a defendant cannot determine guilt.

Conclusion

Bangladesh’s ICT began as a long-delayed effort to prosecute 1971 atrocities, but its history has become inseparable from debates about law, memory, political power, and state violence. International observers’ central lesson is not to abandon accountability. It is to make accountability credible: prosecute according to evidence, apply clear law, protect defence rights and witnesses, permit scrutiny, publish reasoned judgments, and investigate allegations of state violence without presuming guilt.

Justice for mass atrocities is strongest when it is both meaningful to victims and demonstrably fair to the accused.
