Can the Subaltern Woman Speak?

Labour, Representation, and the Politics of Listening

This original argument brings Spivak, Marxist feminism and feminist epistemology into dialogue. “Subaltern” is not a synonym for every person experiencing poverty or discrimination. The contemporary examples are constructed for analysis, not reports of particular incidents.

A woman says that she is exhausted. Her family advises patience. She says that she cannot continue carrying every household responsibility. She is reminded that family life requires sacrifice. A worker challenges her conditions; her manners and loyalty are examined before her complaint.

In these situations, speech has occurred. What remains unsettled is how that speech has been received. Has exhaustion become a claim for redistribution, or merely an emotional condition? Has objection been recognised as a decision, or treated as disobedience requiring correction?

The question of the subaltern woman’s speech concerns her capacity to speak and the power to determine what her speech means and what follows from it.

1. Spivak: through whose interpretation does speech become visible?

Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak’s “Can the Subaltern Speak?” examines a problem of representation: those claiming to disclose oppressed people’s experiences may also subordinate those experiences to their own explanations. The subaltern woman is already central to her essay. Colonial rescue narratives and indigenous patriarchal authority can both displace women’s own positions. [1]

The question developed here is whether a woman’s account can change the terms of a discussion. Must her interpretation first receive approval from a family authority, expert or political representative?

Women do not occupy a single subaltern position. Class, property, education, caste, ethnicity and institutional connections differentiate them. A woman may experience domination in one relationship while exercising authority over an employee in another. Any claim about women’s voices must therefore specify which women, addressing whom, within which relations of power.

2. A Marxist question: what are the material conditions of speaking?

A formal opportunity to express an opinion does not provide everyone with an equal practical ability to use it. Someone whose housing, income or children’s security depends on another person’s decisions faces a different potential cost of objection.

In Capital, Marx examines production as a continuing process of reproduction through which the relation between labour and capital is renewed. [2] The original question proposed here is: which relationships are reproduced through expectations of women’s silence?

Consider a woman who undertakes nearly all household care. Her work makes time available for other people’s employment, education or rest. When she asks for responsibilities to be shared, she also questions the distribution of time and opportunity.

Taking her statement seriously may require another person to surrender an advantage. Expressing appreciation can be easier than assuming part of her workload.

Listening therefore has a material dimension: whose time, resources or authority must change if the claim is accepted?

3. Marxist feminism: who reproduces labour-power?

Lise Vogel’s social-reproduction approach locates women’s oppression within the organisation of the reproduction of life and labour-power under capitalism. Nancy Fraser examines how capital accumulation depends on social reproduction while potentially undermining its conditions. [3–4]

Reproduction here includes more than childbirth. Feeding people, providing care, making rest possible and raising the next generation also enter the analysis.

This perspective challenges the treatment of the household as a natural space outside the economy. Nevertheless, saying that unpaid domestic labour sustains economic life is different from claiming that all such labour directly produces surplus value in Marx’s technical sense. Those propositions should not be conflated.

The argument here is narrower: if care is necessary to sustain social life, caregivers’ claims about its organisation should carry weight in collective decisions.

4. Does economic recognition secure freedom?

Monetary estimates of household services can challenge the statement that a homemaker does no work. But must a woman demonstrate the monetary value of her output before her need for rest deserves recognition?

Labour valuation and personal dignity must remain distinguishable. If greater productivity establishes a stronger entitlement to freedom, where does that leave people who are ill, older or unable to work?

A family can acknowledge a woman’s contribution while refusing to share responsibilities. A state can publish the value of unpaid care without securing caregivers’ control over resources. Recognition therefore requires examination of its consequences: can she decide how to use her time, decline a responsibility or demand its redistribution?

Praise can initiate change. It can also become a substitute for change.

5. Feminist epistemology: who is recognised as credible?

Miranda Fricker describes testimonial injustice as an unfair credibility deficit resulting from prejudice. Her account of hermeneutical injustice concerns disadvantages arising from prejudicial gaps in shared resources for understanding social experience. [5]

These concepts illuminate a possible situation: a woman identifies harassment, but a listener dismisses her as excessively emotional. Alternatively, an institution may classify repeated humiliation only as demanding management, leaving the harm inadequately understood.

However, it would also be mistaken to assume that marginalised people cannot interpret their experiences. Women may develop clear explanations within their own communities that powerful institutions refuse to accept. The problem may involve resistance to existing knowledge as well as inadequacies in shared interpretive resources.

6. Does one woman’s empowerment emancipate another?

Chandra Talpade Mohanty challenges representations of the “Third World woman” as a homogeneous, helpless figure detached from specific histories and social relations. Her work calls for attention to differentiated positions. [6]

That caution matters in Bangladesh too. One woman’s professional opportunities may be supported by another woman’s inexpensive domestic labour. The first woman’s independence can be real while the second woman’s restricted opportunities remain real. Neither needs to be denied to investigate the other.

Women’s presence in leadership matters, but it does not by itself establish representation of all women. Who makes decisions, who performs the work and who can challenge an arrangement remain distinct questions.

7. Necessary representation, accountable representation

Lawyers, researchers, journalists and organisers can strengthen marginalised women’s claims. Abandoning representation altogether is neither a sufficient nor a practical response. Yet access to someone’s account does not confer unlimited authority over its meaning.

A researcher selects experiences for analysis. An editor removes material. An organiser decides which demands can wait. These choices require explanation and opportunities for challenge.

Accountable representation allows the woman concerned to contest an interpretation. Her consent matters when disclosure carries risks. Disagreement with a representative should not automatically jeopardise access to assistance.

This does not place anyone’s statements beyond evidential scrutiny. Evaluating a claim differs from presuming that its speaker is unreliable because of her identity or status. Fair procedures can hear competing accounts and examine evidence.

8. What institutions would make listening consequential?

The normative proposal developed here is to assess participation through influence over decisions as well as opportunities to address a meeting.

Can the woman help determine the agenda? Does she have time, safe transport and alternative care arrangements enabling participation? Can she disagree without fearing loss of income or membership? Can she appeal a decision?

Without such conditions, participatory arrangements may preserve existing power. Attendance and effective political authority are not equivalent.

Conclusion

Subaltern women speak, reason, refuse, negotiate and organise. The task is not to imagine agency on their behalf. It is to identify the relationships through which agency becomes constrained or its meaning displaced.

This essay argues that economic dependence, unequal care responsibilities and prejudicial credibility judgments can jointly restrict the consequences of women’s speech. Addressing that problem requires opportunities for representation, material support and accountable decision-making together.

Alongside “Can the subaltern woman speak?” we must therefore ask:

When she speaks, how willing are we to let her words change our advantages and authority?
