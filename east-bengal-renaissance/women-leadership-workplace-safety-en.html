Do women feel safer at work under a female boss?

How women’s workplace experiences change when more women enter leadership is a question about representation, power and institutional accountability. Employees may expect a female boss to understand their concerns or take harassment complaints seriously. Expectations, actual support and experiences of harassment, however, are distinct. Selected studies identify benefits associated with women’s leadership, alongside mixed findings across different outcomes.

The evidence supports a qualified conclusion: women’s leadership can contribute to safer and more inclusive workplaces. A supervisor’s gender alone does not establish employees’ safety; leadership behaviour, effective policies and accountability also need assessment.

1. What does “feeling safe” mean?

At least three outcomes need to be distinguished. The first is exposure to sexual harassment, threats or gender-based humiliation. The second is psychological safety: whether employees can ask questions, acknowledge mistakes or express disagreement without fearing humiliation or punishment. The third is trust in complaint procedures, including confidentiality and protection against retaliation. Job satisfaction and intentions to leave may relate to these outcomes, but they are not interchangeable measures.

Consequently, finding that women are less likely to leave under female leadership does not directly establish that they are safer from sexual harassment. Similarly, fewer formal complaints do not necessarily mean less harassment: barriers to reporting can also reduce recorded complaints. This is a methodological caution about interpreting organisational data.

2. Women’s supervisory representation and sexual harassment

Liu and Riccucci’s Women’s Representation and Federal Employees’ Sexual Harassment Experience examines U.S. federal workplaces. A greater proportion of women supervisors was associated with a lower likelihood of female employees experiencing sexual harassment. Women’s representation in workgroups and the perceived effectiveness of anti-harassment policies were also associated with less harassment. The article first appeared online in 2025 and was assigned to a 2026 journal issue. [1]

This is not simply a comparison between one particular female boss and one particular male boss. Supervisory representation is an organisational characteristic. Other supportive conditions may accompany greater representation, so the association should not be presented as the isolated causal effect of appointing a female supervisor.

3. Benefits of female leadership do not imply uniformly better experiences

Alan, Çörekçioğlu, Kaba and Sutter analysed information on more than 2,000 professionals in 24 large corporations in Türkiye. Female employees received more support and were less likely to leave under female leadership. Professional networks were also less segregated by gender. [2]

However, women working under female leaders also reported worse workplace satisfaction and perceptions of meritocracy. Their assessments of workplace climate were particularly negative when support from their female leader was absent. Receiving support, remaining in a job and feeling satisfied are therefore distinct outcomes. These findings do not justify generalising that all women leaders are more—or less—empathetic. The article appeared online in 2025 and in its final journal issue in 2026. [2]

4. Supportive supervision and psychological safety

Halliday and colleagues examined relationships among perceived supervisor support, psychological safety and turnover intentions using data from 5,578 research and development employees across 24 countries. Their analysis also considered employee gender and national gender-equality contexts. [3]

The study addresses how supportive supervision relates to employees’ experiences. It should not be treated as a direct test of the claim that female bosses are safer than male bosses. Conflating supportive behaviour with a supervisor’s gender obscures the question being investigated.

5. Women supervisors’ own safety matters

McLaughlin, Uggen and Blackstone’s 2012 study used longitudinal survey data and qualitative interviews. Women supervisors reported more harassment than women without supervisory authority. The authors interpret harassment as something that can also be used to undermine women’s authority. [4]

Formal authority therefore does not automatically remove exposure to gender-based hostility. But a finding about harassment experienced by women supervisors does not establish whether women reporting to those supervisors are more or less safe. These are separate research questions.

6. Limits of the evidence

This article is a narrative review of selected studies, rather than a systematic review or meta-analysis of all relevant evidence. U.S. government offices, large Turkish corporations and research and development employees across countries do not represent every office or factory in Bangladesh.

Causal interpretation also requires care. An organisation that is already inclusive may have both more women leaders and safer working conditions. Employees are not always randomly assigned to teams or supervisors. Self-reported experiences are important, but recognition, recall and willingness to disclose harassment may differ. No universal percentage increase in safety under female bosses can responsibly be inferred from this selection of studies.

7. Proposals relevant to workplaces in Bangladesh

The following are policy proposals informed by this review. They are not presented as interventions already demonstrated to work in Bangladesh or as an organisation’s formally adopted position.

  • Give women meaningful decision-making authority. Fair access to leadership and promotion should be accompanied by actual authority over budgets, personnel and policy enforcement.
  • Provide multiple reporting routes. Employees need a channel beyond their immediate supervisor, including when that supervisor is the subject of a complaint.
  • Protect confidentiality and address retaliation. Limit information to those who need it and review allegations of punitive transfers, humiliation, removal of work or retaliatory evaluations.
  • Ensure fair, independent investigations. Require recusal where conflicts of interest arise, hear both complainants and respondents, and provide evidence-based decisions and an appeal route.
  • Evaluate leadership behaviour. Listening to employees, intervening against disrespect and conducting fair evaluations should form part of a supervisor’s performance assessment.

Organisations could use anonymous surveys to assess harassment experiences, safety in speaking up, trust in complaint procedures and fear of retaliation separately. Reporting must avoid identifying people in small groups. Complaint counts should be interpreted alongside employees’ experiences and the quality of case handling.

8. Conclusion

There is evidence linking women’s representation in leadership with less sexual harassment and greater support. There is no guarantee that a female boss will make women employees feel safe. Assessing an institution requires asking who holds leadership positions, how they exercise power and how the organisation responds when harm is reported.
