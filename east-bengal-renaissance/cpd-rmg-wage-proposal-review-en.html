Garment Wages: Limits of CPD’s Estimate and the Case for Tk 30,000

East Bengal Renaissance · November 23, 2026

On November 18, the Centre for Policy Dialogue (CPD) proposed an indicative monthly wage range of Tk 18,578 to Tk 24,204 for Bangladesh’s ready-made garment workers. CPD calculates Tk 18,578 as the income needed to keep a four-person family above the urban poverty line, and Tk 24,204 as its estimate of a “living wage” for basic but decent living. CPD itself says the estimates rely on secondary data and are sensitive to assumptions about household size and the number of earners. The range should not be treated as a final or precise wage formula. Read CPD’s full presentation and calculations.

What underlies CPD’s calculations?

CPD presents three separate calculations. First, it adjusts the Tk 12,500 minimum wage set in 2023 by the urban consumer price index and obtains Tk 16,351 for October 2026. This restores the old purchasing power after inflation; it does not improve living standards. Second, CPD updates the 2022 Dhaka urban upper poverty line using CPI, calculates the cost for a four-person household, and divides it by 1.58 earners to obtain Tk 18,578. Third, it estimates a family budget of Tk 38,243 by adding food, other essentials, rent, and 5% for emergencies and savings. Dividing this by 1.58 gives Tk 24,204 per worker.

This framework is useful for discussion. But unless its components and assumptions can be checked, the result should not be treated as a definitive “living wage.”

The same household size, but a large gap in living costs

The Global Living Wage Coalition’s 2025 Anker benchmark for Dhaka City estimates a monthly cost of Tk 48,022 for a basic but decent life for a family of four, and a living-wage benchmark of Tk 30,459 per worker. CPD uses the same family size but estimates a household cost of Tk 38,243 and Tk 24,204 per worker. The worker-level gap between the two benchmarks is Tk 6,255; the family-cost gap is Tk 9,779. The food basket, rent, other essentials, and savings calculations should be published side by side to explain this difference. Dhaka City living-wage benchmark.

The studies differ in timing and method: the Anker benchmark is for 2025, while CPD’s figures are adjusted by CPI through October 2026. Putting the numbers next to each other does not prove that CPD’s estimate is wrong. It does show why the methods and components behind the gap need a transparent explanation.

How stable is the assumption of 1.58 earners?

CPD says studies report RMG household sizes ranging from 3.6 to 4.2 members, and earners ranging from 1.37 to 2.1. It uses the median from five sources: four household members and 1.58 earners. A median can be a reasonable summary, but it is not a substitute for a representative survey of garment-worker households. CPD acknowledges that no large, representative survey of this kind is available.

To see the effect of the assumption, keep CPD’s family budget of Tk 38,243 unchanged. Dividing it by 1.37 earners gives about Tk 27,914 per worker; dividing by 2.1 gives about Tk 18,211. Even with the same household budget, changing only the number of earners moves the living-wage estimate by nearly Tk 9,700. The Wage Board should commission a recent, representative worker survey rather than treating 1.58 as an established fact.

One inconsistency in the presentation needs clarification

One slide in CPD’s presentation describes the model food basket as providing 2,188 kilocalories per day; the detailed living-wage calculation says 2,118. CPD should clarify which value it used. The detailed table also gives no source for the Tk 10,000 rent assumption. Its estimate for other essentials uses a ratio from urban households around the 40th percentile of HIES 2022. Actual housing costs and family expenses should be measured for workers in the industrial areas where they live.

Being above the poverty line is not the same as living with dignity

Tk 18,578 is CPD’s estimate of the amount needed to keep a family above the urban upper poverty line. That is an important minimum protection benchmark, but it does not by itself ensure a dignified standard of living. Treating Tk 18,578–24,204 as the final boundary for wage negotiations risks confining workers’ needs to the poverty line and a limited household budget.

The industry’s ability to pay also matters. But CPD’s presentation says the Board lacks a recent large worker survey and the complete evidence needed to assess industry capacity. With those gaps, it is not enough to present one living-wage estimate and also treat it as a realistic upper limit. The process needs published evidence on productivity by factory size, profits, order prices, costs, and employment.

Why we propose Tk 30,000

The Global Living Wage Coalition’s 2025 Dhaka City benchmark is Tk 30,459. East Bengal Renaissance’s Tk 30,000 proposal is close to that published benchmark and above the Tk 25,166 benchmark for the industrial areas surrounding Dhaka. The figure is therefore not arbitrary; it is a policy proposal grounded in a published cost-of-living estimate. Read East Bengal Renaissance’s full wage proposal.

Still, the Dhaka City benchmark cannot be treated as an exact measure of workers’ needs in every industrial area. New surveys should capture regional living costs and the realities of workers’ households. That limitation does not make the Tk 30,000 demand invalid; it strengthens the case for transparent, representative, regularly updated data in wage-setting.

Our position

We welcome CPD’s call for evidence-based discussion. But its Tk 18,578–24,204 range should not be accepted as the final boundary of a fair wage. The Minimum Wage Board should publish new representative surveys, regional living costs, grade-based wage structures, and evidence on factories’ actual capacity. International buyers should also state how much of higher wages they will include in their order prices. Workers’ living costs must be measured carefully; that measurement must not become a seal of approval for keeping wages low.
