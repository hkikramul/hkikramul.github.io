Does the Qur’an command killing nonbelievers?

Qur’an 9:5, its historical setting, and debates in classical and modern interpretation

A source-based review · First published November 23, 2026

ملخص بالعربية:

هل يأمر القرآن بقتل غير المسلمين لمجرد اختلافهم في الدين؟ تتناول هذه المقالة الآية ٩:٥ في سياق الآيات ٩:١–٦، وتعرض قراءات المفسرين والباحثين، مع التمييز بين نص القرآن وسياقه التاريخي والتفسير الفقهي اللاحق.

Short answer: The Qur’an contains forceful commands to fight and kill in wartime passages; Qur’an 9:5 is one example. But reading it as a universal command to kill anyone who is not Muslim does not account for the surrounding verses or the history of interpretation. At the same time, some later interpreters extended the scope of fighting beyond a particular conflict. A careful answer must distinguish the verse, its historical setting, and later interpretation.

First, the wording: whom does 9:5 name?

Qur’an 9:5 uses الْمُشْرِكِينَ (al-mushrikīn), commonly translated “polytheists” or “those who associate partners with God.” It does not use the word kāfir in this verse. Translating all religious difference as “unbelievers” or the more polemical English word “infidels” can blur important distinctions. Qur’anic terminology, a translator’s English or Bengali choice, and the historical identity of a group are not interchangeable.

Read 9:5 with its passage: verses 9:1–6

The opening passage of Surah al-Tawbah discusses treaties, conflict, a period of notice, and protection for a person seeking asylum. The Arabic text and English sense translations of verses 9:4–6 appear below. These English and Bengali renderings are paraphrases prepared for this article; published translations may use different wording.

9:4 — Honour treaties

إِلَّا الَّذِينَ عَاهَدتُّم مِّنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ ثُمَّ لَمْ يَنقُصُوكُمْ شَيْئًا وَلَمْ يُظَاهِرُوا عَلَيْكُمْ أَحَدًا فَأَتِمُّوا إِلَيْهِمْ عَهْدَهُمْ إِلَىٰ مُدَّتِهِمْ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُحِبُّ الْمُتَّقِينَ

English sense translation: As for the polytheists with whom you made a treaty, if they have not broken it or aided an enemy against you, honour the treaty until its term ends.

বাংলা ভাবানুবাদ: মুশরিকদের মধ্যে যাদের সঙ্গে তোমাদের চুক্তি আছে, তারা যদি চুক্তি ভঙ্গ না করে এবং তোমাদের বিরুদ্ধে কাউকে সাহায্য না করে, তবে নির্ধারিত মেয়াদ পর্যন্ত তাদের সঙ্গে চুক্তি পূর্ণ করো।

9:5 — Forceful language about fighting

فَإِذَا انْسَلَخَ الْأَشْهُرُ الْحُرُمُ فَاقْتُلُوا الْمُشْرِكِينَ حَيْثُ وَجَدتُّمُوهُمْ وَخُذُوهُمْ وَاحْصُرُوهُمْ وَاقْعُدُوا لَهُمْ كُلَّ مَرْصَدٍ ۚ فَإِن تَابُوا وَأَقَامُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَآتَوُا الزَّكَاةَ فَخَلُّوا سَبِيلَهُمْ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌ

English sense translation: When the sacred months have passed, kill the polytheists wherever you find them; seize them, besiege them, and lie in wait for them at every watchpost. But if they repent, establish prayer, and give alms, let them go their way. God is forgiving and merciful.

বাংলা ভাবানুবাদ: নিষিদ্ধ মাসগুলো অতিবাহিত হলে মুশরিকদের বিরুদ্ধে যুদ্ধ করো—তাদের যেখানে পাও, তাদের আটক করো, অবরোধ করো এবং তাদের জন্য অপেক্ষা করো। তারা তওবা করে, নামাজ কায়েম করে ও যাকাত দেয়, তবে তাদের পথ ছেড়ে দাও। নিশ্চয়ই আল্লাহ ক্ষমাশীল, দয়ালু।

Textual clarity matters: The phrase fa-qtulū is an imperative meaning “kill.” Its forceful wording should not be denied or softened away. The interpretive questions are whom “the polytheists” refers to here, which conflict the passage addresses, and how far the command was understood to apply in later periods.

9:6 — Give protection and escort to safety

وَإِنْ أَحَدٌ مِّنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ اسْتَجَارَكَ فَأَجِرْهُ حَتَّىٰ يَسْمَعَ كَلَامَ اللَّهِ ثُمَّ أَبْلِغْهُ مَأْمَنَهُ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ قَوْمٌ لَّا يَعْلَمُونَ

English sense translation: If a polytheist asks you for protection, grant it so that the person may hear God’s message; then escort that person to a place of safety.

বাংলা ভাবানুবাদ: মুশরিকদের কেউ যদি তোমার কাছে আশ্রয় চায়, তাকে আশ্রয় দাও, যাতে সে আল্লাহর বাণী শুনতে পারে; তারপর তাকে তার নিরাপদ স্থানে পৌঁছে দাও।

Read together, these verses contain both the severe wartime language of 9:5 and instructions to honour treaties (9:4) and protect an asylum-seeker (9:6). Treating 9:5 as an unrestricted licence for violence against all non-Muslims is not a reading of the whole passage.

Historical setting: what can be said, and what remains reconstruction?

The opening of Surah al-Tawbah announces the termination of certain agreements, allows a period of notice, exempts treaty partners who remain faithful, and addresses asylum. Later Muslim historical and exegetical accounts connect the passage with treaty conflicts in seventh-century Arabia. Some reports associate the breakdown with the Hudaybiyya treaty and clashes involving allied tribes. Qur’an 9:5 itself, however, does not name a tribe. Historical reconstruction therefore requires distinguishing the Qur’anic text from hadith, sira narratives, and later tafsir, while assessing each source’s date, purpose, and reliability.

It is better to identify the source when claiming that the verse refers to one named event. Verses 9:4–6 themselves establish the treaty-and-protection setting; fuller accounts of the particular events depend on later historical sources.

Classical exegesis was not a single, unchanging view

In her survey of early interpretations in The Oxford Handbook of Qur’anic Studies, Qur’an scholar Asma Afsaruddin examines how influential commentators such as al-Tabari, al-Zamakhshari, and al-Qurtubi treated verses about fighting. Her account describes substantial diversity. Many pre-Mamluk interpreters connected 9:5 to seventh-century Arabian polytheists without a treaty or who had broken one; they did not agree that 9:5 abrogated Qur’anic counsel to maintain good relations with peaceful people of other faiths.

That history was not uniform. Afsaruddin also describes later medieval interpretations that came to identify 9:5 as “the verse of the sword” and used it to support a broader mandate to fight groups treated as enemies of Islam. Across time, interpreters developed both historically limited readings and readings that extended the scope of the command. Neither tendency should be hidden from readers.

Modern scholarship and the value—and limits—of contextual reading

M. A. S. Abdel Haleem, Qur’an translator and professor at SOAS, argues in “The Role of Context in Interpreting and Translating the Qur’an” that surrounding verses and the larger surah matter for translation and interpretation. He reads 9:5 within 9:1–6 and the treaty conflict described there, rather than as a general command against everyone outside Islam. This is a clear modern scholarly position, but it should not be presented as the only interpretation ever held.

Afsaruddin’s contribution is to examine not only contemporary contextual readings but also changes and disagreements across the history of tafsir. Her work cautions against claims that “all Muslim scholars in every period agreed” on one interpretation. It also cautions against the opposite simplification that every contextual reading is merely an attempt to excuse the text.

What other Qur’anic verses add

  • Al-Baqarah 2:190 connects fighting with “those who fight you” and prohibits transgression.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:256 states that there is no compulsion in religion, though interpreters have discussed the scope of this principle.
  • Al-Mumtahanah 60:8 says God does not forbid kindness and justice toward those who have not fought Muslims on account of religion or driven them from their homes.

It would be too simple to claim that these verses automatically cancel 9:5. It would also be incomplete to treat 9:5 as the only relevant verse. How these passages relate involves tafsir, the theory of abrogation (naskh), and jurisprudence; there have been historical disagreements about these questions.

A careful conclusion

The most accurate short answer has two parts:

  1. The Qur’an contains a direct command about killing and fighting polytheists in 9:5. Its forceful wording should be quoted honestly.
  2. Presenting 9:5 as a universal command to kill anyone who is not Muslim leaves out the treaty and asylum context of 9:1–6 and the diversity of historical interpretation. Some later interpretations and legal traditions did extend the scope of fighting beyond that particular conflict; that history should also be acknowledged.

A careful formulation is therefore: The Qur’an contains wartime commands to kill; the intended group and scope of those commands have been interpreted differently across history. An isolated translation cannot stand for the whole Qur’an or for all Muslims. Scriptural interpretation, state law, and an individual’s act of violence are separate questions. This article is not a fatwa; it aims to distinguish the text, history, and interpretive traditions.
