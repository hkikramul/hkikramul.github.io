TEACHING PHILOSOPHY

From a meaningful question to a defensible conclusion.

My goal is to help students become thoughtful users of data: able to ask a clear question, examine how evidence was collected, recognize variability, and explain what an analysis can and cannot establish. I connect intuitive explanations and visual representations with mathematical reasoning, so that procedures have a purpose and conclusions remain grounded in context.

I aim to organize statistics discussions around investigation: question, data, exploration, inference, and communication. Real examples from health and everyday life can make decisions about sampling, measurement, confounding, and missing data visible. Simulation offers a bridge from informal ideas about chance to sampling distributions, randomization tests, and confidence intervals; formulas then deepen that understanding.

A supportive classroom also needs intellectual challenge. I encourage questions and treat mistakes as opportunities to diagnose reasoning. My teaching development priorities include structured peer discussion, brief checks of understanding, scaffolded computing, and clear feedback. I seek to make participation accessible through readable materials, multiple representations, and opportunities to think before speaking.

Assessment should reveal understanding as well as procedural accuracy. I aim to align tasks with explicit learning goals and ask students to justify a method, interpret uncertainty, and critique a conclusion. For example, a correct calculation should be accompanied by an explanation of the population, assumptions, and limits of the inference. Short written explanations and transfer problems can expose misconceptions that a numerical answer alone misses.

Responsible analysis is part of statistical literacy. My teaching goals include reproducible workflows, transparent data provenance, privacy, and a careful distinction between association and causation. Where course policy permits AI tools, students should disclose their use, verify outputs, and explain the reasoning themselves. Technology should support learning without replacing judgment.

I view teaching as an ongoing process of inquiry. I plan to use student questions, formative assessments, peer feedback, and reflective notes to refine discussion activities with the course instructor. These commitments draw on ASA GAISE guidance and statistics education research; the approaches below describe my continuing development rather than a claim that every method has already been implemented.
