The Evolution of Western Mathematics

Biggan Jiggasha

The same question in different ages

Why is a geometric rule true? Where will a planet be next month? How can you give a precise answer about an infinite process? Across the Mediterranean and Europe, the language used to answer these questions changed several times. Geometry supplied an ideal of proof. Commerce, navigation and astronomy created demand for new techniques. [1]

A language of proof in the ancient Mediterranean

Euclid’s Elements, compiled in Greek around 300 BCE, became an influential model of reasoning step by step from definitions and postulates to theorems. Ancient mathematics also pursued many other questions. Archimedes used geometric bounds to calculate areas and volumes. One idea behind his method can be pictured by trapping an area between simpler shapes drawn inside and outside its boundary. [1,2]

When methods cross languages

Many Greek works were translated into Arabic in the Middle Ages. Arabic-writing scholars also studied Indian calculation systems and developed new mathematics. Al-Khwarizmi’s algebra, with rules for rearranging and solving equations, is one example. Latin translations widened access in Europe from the twelfth century onward. Fibonacci, educated in Mediterranean trading centers, used Hindu–Arabic numerals to work through currency conversion, profit and other problems in his Liber Abaci (1202). The notation gradually gained ground alongside established accounting practices. [1,3,4]

Calculating motion and change

Coordinate geometry made it possible to describe curves with equations. In the seventeenth century, Newton and Leibniz developed calculus by different routes. If a moving object’s position changes over time, a derivative tells you how fast it is changing at an instant. An integral can recover the total displacement from the velocity over an interval. Newton’s Principia (1687), written largely in geometric arguments, showed what could be gained by treating motion and gravitation in one mathematical framework. [1,5]

Turning the questions on axioms and proof

In the nineteenth century, non-Euclidean geometries showed that changing the assumption about parallel lines could yield coherent alternatives. In analysis, precise definitions of limits and convergence clarified the foundations of calculus. Universities, professional societies and journals offered new settings for checking and circulating these ideas. Hilbert’s list of problems in 1900 illustrates how this research culture could set an agenda. Both habits continue today: expressing the world in equations and examining the foundations on which the equations rest. [1,6]
