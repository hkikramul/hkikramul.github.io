A History of Number Theory: From Prime Numbers to Digital Signatures

Biggan Jiggasha · November 24, 2026

Three hours after ten at night, the clock reads one. Add ten and three to get thirteen, then divide by twelve and keep the remainder, one. This familiar calculation leads to a central idea of number theory. Group numbers that leave the same remainder, and you begin to see the structure behind problems ranging from calendars to digital signatures.

What can we ask about whole numbers?

Number theory studies questions about divisibility, prime numbers, and equations whose solutions must be integers or rational numbers. A prime is an integer greater than one whose only positive divisors are one and itself. Examples include 2, 3, 5 and 7. The number 84 breaks into 2 × 2 × 3 × 7. Apart from the order of the factors, that prime factorization is unique. Primes reveal the multiplicative structure of the positive integers. [1, 3]

From a Babylonian clay tablet to Euclid’s proof

The ancient Babylonian clay tablet Plimpton 322 contains a table of numbers associated with Pythagorean triples. The numbers 3, 4 and 5 illustrate the idea because 3² + 4² = 5². Scholars have proposed several explanations for what the tablet was used for. Books VII to IX of Euclid’s Elements, compiled around 300 BCE, present a systematic treatment of divisibility and primes. [1–3]

One of Euclid’s arguments is easy to try yourself. Write down any finite list of primes. Multiply them all together and add one. Every prime on the list leaves a remainder of one when used to divide the new number. Yet this new number is greater than one, so it has a prime divisor. That divisor must lie outside the list. No finite list can contain every prime. [3]

Equations in India, China and Alexandria

In his Arithmetica, Diophantus of Alexandria looked for rational solutions to various equations. Many problems about integer solutions later became known as Diophantine equations. In India, Aryabhata’s kuttaka method found integer solutions to certain linear equations. In the seventh century, Brahmagupta also worked on equations involving squares of two variables. [1, 4, 5]

The Chinese mathematical text Sunzi Suanjing poses a problem about remainders. A number leaves remainders of 2, 3 and 2 when divided by 3, 5 and 7, respectively. The smallest positive answer is 23. Add 105 for another answer, since 105 is a multiple of all three divisors. The modern Chinese remainder theorem says that, when the divisors are pairwise coprime, the conditions have a common solution and all solutions are spaced by the product of the divisors. [6]

Gauss, Riemann and Ramanujan

Carl Friedrich Gauss published his Disquisitiones Arithmeticae in 1801 and systematically used the relation between numbers that leave the same remainder. Today we write 13 ≡ 1 (mod 12), since the difference between 13 and 1 is divisible by 12. The notation lets us organize infinitely many integers into a finite set of classes and compute with them. [7]

Gaps between neighboring primes vary. Yet a pattern emerges when we count primes over a long stretch. The prime number theorem says that the number of primes up to a large x is approximately x / ln(x). In 1859, Bernhard Riemann connected the distribution of primes to the zeta function. Resolving his still-open hypothesis would tell us more about how closely the prime count follows its overall trend. [8]

Srinivasa Ramanujan worked on another kind of counting. Ignoring order, there are five ways to write 4 as a sum of positive integers: 4, 3 + 1, 2 + 2, 2 + 1 + 1, and 1 + 1 + 1 + 1. These are called partitions. G. H. Hardy and Ramanujan found a formula that describes how rapidly the partition count grows for large numbers. [9]

How Fermat’s claim led to curves

We can write 3² + 4² = 5². Can three positive integers satisfy the same kind of equation with a whole-number exponent greater than two? Pierre de Fermat claimed they could not. More than three centuries later, work by Andrew Wiles and Richard Taylor completed the proof, and the two related papers appeared in 1995. The argument hinged on a deep connection between elliptic curves and modular forms. Answering a short question about powers brought together two seemingly distant areas of mathematics. [10]

Number theory in digital signatures and QR codes

Digital signatures. Multiplying two large primes is relatively easy. Given the product of two suitably chosen large primes, recovering the factors is difficult with known classical methods. This asymmetry is one idea behind RSA. Schemes such as RSA use modular arithmetic to create digital signatures that can be checked with a public key. Security in practice also depends on the scheme and its correct implementation. [11]

QR codes. A phone can sometimes recover information even when a QR code is dirty or damaged. Reed–Solomon coding adds extra information to the original data. It works over a finite number system with addition, subtraction, multiplication and division by nonzero elements. The extra information allows the decoder to reconstruct the original message when damage stays within the code’s correction capacity. [12]

Try it yourself

A number leaves remainder 2 when divided by 5 and remainder 3 when divided by 7. What is the smallest positive number that works?

Reveal the answer

The numbers that leave remainder 2 when divided by 5 are 2, 7, 12, 17, and so on. Of these, 17 is the first that leaves remainder 3 when divided by 7. The next answer is 52 because adding 5 × 7 changes neither remainder.
