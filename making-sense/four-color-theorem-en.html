Four Colors for Every Map? A New Algorithmic Breakthrough

Biggan Jiggasha · November 23, 2026

Suppose we must color neighboring regions on a flat map so that no two neighbors share a color. What is the largest number of colors needed? Four. But the much-discussed 2026 research did not prove the theorem for the first time. It showed how to find a four-coloring for very large maps much faster than before.

What does the question actually say?

Imagine coloring the districts of Bangladesh on a map. Two districts count as neighbors when they share part of a boundary; merely meeting at one point does not usually count. The Four Color Theorem says every planar map can be colored so that neighboring regions have different colors, using no more than four colors. Some maps need fewer, but three colors do not suffice for every map.

Mathematicians represent a map as a planar graph. Each region becomes a vertex, and each neighboring pair becomes an edge. The question becomes: can each vertex be assigned one of four colors so that the endpoints of every edge have different colors?

The theorem was already proved

The question dates to 1852. Alfred Kempe published a proof in 1879 that was accepted for about eleven years, until Percy Heawood found a flaw. The correct proof came in 1976, when Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken established the theorem with help from a computer checking many cases. Because a crucial part relied on computer calculations, the work also sparked debate about how mathematical proofs should be verified. In the 1990s, Neil Robertson, Daniel Sanders, Paul Seymour and Robin Thomas gave a shorter computer-assisted proof. [1, 2]

Where is the new progress?

The theorem’s truth was settled. The harder question was how quickly an algorithm could find a four-coloring for a particular large graph. A prominent 1996 algorithm took about O(n²) time, where n is the graph’s size, such as its number of vertices. In a simple comparison, making the input ten times larger could make the work about one hundred times greater.

The 2026 FOCS research by Yuta Inoue, Ken-ichi Kawarabayashi, Atsuyuki Miyashita, Bojan Mohar, Carsten Thomassen and Mikkel Thorup reduces this to O(n log n), or near-linear time. The paper was presented at FOCS 2026 and received the conference’s Best Paper Award. [1, 2]

The key is to find small patterns inside a graph that can be simplified—called reducible configurations—along with certain short obstructing cycles. Earlier proofs generally showed that at least one such pattern exists in any map. The new result shows that a large planar graph contains a number of them proportional to the graph’s size. This lets the algorithm handle many parts at once instead of removing one small piece and returning to the entire problem. The researchers also find such patterns in relatively “flat” parts of a graph, where previous methods struggled. [1]

Are O(n log n) and O(n) the same?

No. O(n log n) is called near-linear; O(n) is linear. Both are much better than O(n²) for large inputs, but they are not mathematically identical. In October 2026, a follow-up preprint by the same team presented an O(n)-time algorithm. It uses new data structures to handle several Kempe changes more quickly. The precise timeline is: the FOCS result was O(n log n); a later preprint improved it to O(n). [3]

Popular accounts can blur these stages. When you see “linear time,” check which paper and version is being described, and whether it is the conference result or later work.

What changes in practice?

This result is unlikely to change how most people color ordinary maps tomorrow. Its importance is in theoretical computer science and graph algorithms: it offers a much faster way to compute a valid coloring for enormous maps and raises the question of whether similar simplifiable structures can help with coloring problems on other surfaces or graphs.

So this is not a “new solution to the Four Color Problem”: that problem was solved nearly half a century ago. The new contribution is an algorithmic advance that uses the proof’s structure to find a solution more efficiently by computer. Pure mathematics can progress this way: the answer may already be known to be true, while new mathematics improves the method for finding it.
