Dependent Data: Why Models and Uncertainty Change

Biggan Jiggasha · November 24, 2026

A study contains 2,000 measurements. That sounds like a large sample. But if they come from 500 patients measured four times each, it would be wrong to analyze them as 2,000 completely separate observations. Measurements from the same person are often related. Ignoring those links can distort averages, uncertainty, test results and predictions in new settings.

What does “independent observation” mean?

In simple terms, independence means that one observation does not carry information about another. In practice, many data points share a person, place, time, study or evolutionary history. A patient’s blood pressure today and next month, two students from the same school, air pollution in neighboring villages, or traits of closely related species may be recorded as separate rows but still be connected.

Dependence does not make data unusable. It means the structure belongs in the analysis. If a standard analysis treats every row as independent when the observations are clustered or sequential, the effective information may be smaller than the raw count suggests. Standard errors and confidence intervals can then be wrong; in some settings they become too small and make evidence look stronger than it is.

Common forms of dependence

  • Hierarchical: plots within sites, students within schools, species within evolutionary groups, or multiple effects within studies. Members of a group share an environment or history.
  • Temporal: repeated surveys of the same people, weekly infections in one region, or measurements across seasons. Observations close in time are often more alike.
  • Spatial: nearby areas may share environment, weather, pollution or movement of people. Measurements from neighboring places can therefore be related.
  • Phylogenetic: closely related species inherit traits from common ancestors. Treating their data as independent can discard that history.

These are not mutually exclusive. One dataset may include repeated measurements within people, people clustered in hospitals, and patterns over time.

Distribution and dependence are different questions

Counts, presence or absence, highly uneven spreads, different variances across groups, and extreme values raise questions about the outcome distribution or other model features. But they are not, by themselves, forms of dependence. An outcome can be a count and also depend on time or location. Ask two questions separately: what is the outcome distribution, and how are observations related?

When can different models help?

Mixed-effects models can represent similarity within groups and differences across groups through random effects or a covariance structure. For example, students in different schools may have different starting levels. Linear mixed models handle continuous outcomes; generalized linear mixed models extend the framework to suitable count or binary outcomes. They often estimate conditional effects while holding group and covariate values fixed.

GLS and specialized models can describe specific covariance patterns such as time, space, phylogeny or state-space structure. When scientific knowledge supports a particular dependence mechanism, modeling it directly can be useful. A wrong covariance structure or an overly complex model, however, can make results sensitive and computation costly.

Generalized estimating equations (GEE) estimate regression parameters using a working correlation structure within groups, often targeting a population-average or marginal association. Under suitable conditions, robust sandwich variance estimation can be useful even if the working correlation is not perfect. With only a small number of independent groups, however, robust standard errors can still be unreliable.

Fixed-effects models can control for stable, time-invariant features of each person, place or group. They do not solve every form of dependence or time-varying unmeasured confounding. The right model depends on the comparison and assumptions the study needs.

Do robust standard errors make the model right?

No. Cluster-robust or sandwich standard errors can protect some inferences against covariance misspecification. They do not automatically fix biased coefficients, a wrong mean model, confounding, poor measurement or an incorrectly chosen target population. With few clusters, common robust methods may be too optimistic; small-sample corrections, an appropriate inferential method or cluster-level resampling may be needed.

Resampling should also preserve structure. For school data, resampling schools as clusters may make more sense than drawing students one at a time. For time series, blocks of nearby days may need to stay together. For spatial data, spatial blocking—and, for prediction, holding out new schools, regions or time periods—can provide a more realistic check.

What should a study report?

  1. Explain how observations were generated: repeated measures, clusters, space, time or a combination.
  2. State whether the main goal is description, estimating an effect, prediction or exploration.
  3. Name the estimand: conditional, population-average, within-unit or predictive.
  4. Explain the model and uncertainty method, and the assumptions they require.
  5. Show whether results change across defensible alternatives for dependence, preprocessing, model class or inference.
  6. Specify the population or new setting to which the conclusion should generalize.

No single model fits every dataset. A strong analysis is one in which the scientific question, data structure, target effect and uncertainty all match.
