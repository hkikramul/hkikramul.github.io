01 / Founding vision — A long-term personal aspiration

Scientific excellence.
Service to people.

Conceived by Md. Ikramul Haq Khan

Our purpose

My dream is to nurture researchers whose knowledge, integrity, and compassion help build a healthier, fairer society. Through the proposed institute, I hope to support young Bangladeshi scholars in becoming outstanding researchers, international scientific leaders, and mentors to future generations.

The institute would be an independent, nonpartisan research and intellectual hub dedicated to rigorous scholarship and public benefit. Human dignity, equal opportunity, and responsibility toward society would guide its work.

Developing people through research

Researchers would be at the center of this vision—their intellectual growth, well-being, scientific freedom, and capacity to contribute. We would seek talented, curious, and highly motivated scholars who demonstrate honesty, ethical judgment, and concern for others.

Opportunities would recognize potential alongside achievement. Financial hardship, geographic location, and limited access to education should not prevent a promising scholar from receiving excellent training. Selection would use transparent criteria and recognize differences in prior opportunity.

As resources permit, support would include mentorship, scholarships, structured courses, collaborative projects, computing and data access, research placements, and international partnerships. Fair working conditions and a supportive research culture would be central commitments. Support would expand only as resources can sustain it.

Collaborative science with a humanitarian purpose

The institute would connect statistics, mathematics, biomedical science, clinical research, and public health. Potential research areas include statistical methodology, epidemiology, infectious-disease modeling, and computational biomedical research.

We would encourage work that improves health, addresses inequalities, strengthens public services, and responds to the needs of underserved communities. Fundamental research would also be valued for its contribution to knowledge and its potential to enable future discoveries.

Research priorities would develop through scientific judgment, meaningful participation by relevant communities, and realistic assessment of resources.

Contribution beyond borders

Scholars could live and work anywhere. My hope is that they would sustain meaningful contributions to Bangladesh through research, teaching, mentorship, collaboration, public engagement, or institution building.

The ambition is to create an enduring intellectual community that connects international expertise with opportunities and needs in Bangladesh, while contributing to human well-being globally.

Support would be offered with respect for each scholar’s autonomy, without requiring political agreement, personal loyalty, or a prescribed career path. Contributions to Bangladesh and wider society would be encouraged through meaningful opportunities and shared purpose.

Independence and shared responsibility

The institute would welcome diverse perspectives. Participation would depend on scientific promise, ethical conduct, collaboration, and commitment to public benefit, without requiring political affiliation or loyalty to the founder.

Scientific questions, methods, and conclusions would remain independent of partisan interests, donor preferences, and personal beliefs. Researchers would be free to disagree, challenge assumptions, and publish findings that contradict expectations.

Transparent governance, responsible use of resources, fair recognition of contributions, and accountability to the people affected by our work would support this independence. Researchers would have ways to question decisions and report mistreatment without retaliation, including concerns involving the founder.

Our long-term ambition

Success would mean generations of scholars who advance knowledge, improve lives, and create opportunities for others. Each researcher we support could become a mentor and institution builder, extending the benefits of the institute beyond its own boundaries.

The ultimate goal is a sustainable intellectual home where scientific excellence and service to people grow together.

Development timeline

This remains a personal, long-term vision. Active development is intended to begin after I secure a tenure-track academic position and assess the necessary collaborators, institutional support, resources, and permissions. No current recruitment, fundraising, or institutional launch is planned.

Our namesake / দেবব্রত বসু

A legacy of
statistical thinking.

Honoring Debabrata Basu (1924–2001), a pioneer in the foundations of statistical inference.

Born in Dhaka, in present-day Bangladesh, Basu became internationally known for his contributions to statistical theory. Basu’s theorem connects complete sufficiency and ancillarity—a lasting part of statistical education.

His intellectual legacy inspires this proposed institute’s commitment to careful reasoning, mathematical depth, and independent inquiry. Biomedical research is the institute’s intended application and collaborative mission.

Learn more: IMS centenary conference · Basu’s theorem at Berkeley · Biographical background.
The name is an intended tribute; this project does not claim endorsement by Basu’s family or former institutions.

English / Original reflection

The courage to question, the discipline to reason

Remembering Debabrata Basu means remembering a tradition of examining the foundations of statistics. Basu’s theorem establishes independence between a complete sufficient statistic and an ancillary statistic, and remains an important part of statistical inference education.

This proposed initiative seeks inspiration from that intellectual legacy: making assumptions explicit, examining the reasoning behind methods, and remaining accountable to evidence. Honoring him would mean putting those commitments into practice in statistics and biomedical research.

An original, source-informed reflection prepared for this website, corresponding to the Bangla text. It is not a quotation from Basu or a previously published tribute, and does not claim family endorsement.

Background for these reflections: Berkeley course notes on Basu’s theorem and the memorial references above. No independently published Bangla remembrance was verified during preparation.

04 / Future development roadmap

Build the research.
Then build the institution.

Begin as a small research initiative, seek an established institutional host, and pursue independence when the science, team, and funding justify it.

The institute is a long-term personal aspiration. Active development is intended to begin after a tenure-track appointment and assessment of resources, permissions, and institutional support. No institutional hosting agreement, founding group, or pilot project is announced. The milestones below describe a possible future path.

01 / Define

A clear founding concept

Prepare a two-page proposal covering the mission, a narrow research focus, public benefit, governance principles, available resources, and conditions for incorporation.

02 / Connect

A dependable founding group

Seek feedback from trusted researchers and identify three to five collaborators with complementary scientific and administrative expertise. Advisory roles would precede formal trustee appointments.

03 / Demonstrate

One hosted pilot

Seek an established university, research center, or nonprofit to host a focused project under written agreements covering responsibilities, finances, ethics, data, and publications.

04 / Establish

An independent organization

Choose a jurisdiction and appoint independent trustees when committed funding, active research, and accountable administrative support make a separate organization worthwhile.

05 / Sustain

Grow against real needs

Add staff, research groups, and facilities only as funded scientific needs emerge and existing operations become stable and independently reviewed.

The first 90 days

An illustrative planning framework, beginning when development work starts—not a fixed launch commitment. Estimated planning effort: 12–20 hours in total, excluding research.

WEEKS 1–2

Write the concept

Develop a two-page founding proposal with a distinctive scientific purpose and a feasible first-year objective.

WEEKS 3–4

Refine with feedback

Ask two trusted researchers to assess the mission, scientific value, feasibility, and potential duplication.

MONTH 2

Explore partnerships

Identify prospective collaborators and discuss hosting a specific project with a suitable institution.

MONTH 3

Design the pilot

Define one research question, deliverables, roles, resources, oversight requirements, and a realistic stopping point.

A candidate starting theme

Uncertainty in infectious-disease models

A possible initial direction connects statistical theory, simulation, and biomedical applications. An illustrative question is how reliably selected probability distributions describe simulated epidemic outcomes across parameter settings.

This is a discussion topic, not an announced institute project. Existing university research remains under its applicable institutional arrangements; any separate collaboration requires agreement and a clear additional contribution.

Before a pilot begins

Agree on the essentials

  • A named host and accountable investigator.
  • Authorized financial administration and an agreed budget.
  • Scientific review, ethics oversight, and data permissions.
  • Responsibilities, authorship process, publication rights, and attribution.
  • Permission to use institutional names and affiliations.
  • Conditions for ending or transitioning the collaboration.

Choose the legal home when the need is clear

A U.S. base remains an option if sustained fundraising, trustees, and administrative capacity develop there. A Bangladesh base may be more practical if leadership and operations are primarily local. Two entities would be considered only when their benefits justify maintaining both systems. No jurisdiction has been selected; applicable legal requirements would be assessed before registration.

Success in an initial year could mean a clear charter, an agreed institutional host, a reliable collaboration, and one completed research output. Across any future structure, the intended safeguards are independent oversight, traceable spending, conflict-of-interest rules, external financial review, and protected scientific freedom.
