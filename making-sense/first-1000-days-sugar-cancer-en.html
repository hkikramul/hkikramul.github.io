Less Sugar in the First 1,000 Days: What a Study Found About Cancer and Aging

Biggan Jiggasha · November 24, 2026

Could food in early childhood be connected with health decades later? Researchers used the end of postwar sugar rationing in the United Kingdom as a natural experiment. Adults who experienced a lower-sugar environment early in life had lower rates of several cancers and differences in some biological aging markers. The findings matter, but they do not predict the future health of any individual child.

A clue in an old policy

Sugar and sweets were rationed in the United Kingdom from the 1940s until September 1953. After rationing ended, historical food data show that average adult sugar consumption rose from about 41 grams a day to nearly 80 grams. Children under two did not receive a separate sugar allowance, but that does not mean they never ate sugar: food was shared within households.[2]

The study asked whether differences in the sugar environment during pregnancy and a child’s first two years, created by the timing of rationing, were related to health in adulthood. This “first 1,000 days” convention covers the period from conception to a child’s second birthday.

How did the researchers compare people?

Chen Zhu and Weilong Zhang analyzed 64,761 UK Biobank participants born in the United Kingdom between 1951 and 1956. Of these, 40,979 had at least part of their first 1,000 days during rationing. The researchers grouped birth cohorts by how long they were exposed to rationing and linked them to later health records from hospitals, cancer registries and mortality data.[1]

This was not a randomized trial. But because rationing ended nationwide on a specific date, comparing people conceived just before and after its end can function as a natural experiment. The authors also checked for pre-existing cohort trends and used type 1 diabetes as a placebo outcome. Still, they did not measure each participant’s exact sugar intake as a baby; exposure was estimated from birth dates and the policy timeline.

Which cancers had lower rates?

Hazard ratios (HRs) were lower for five cancers among the cohorts with longer rationing exposure. These comparisons show the longest-exposed groups relative to a never-rationed comparison group; 95% confidence intervals are in parentheses.

  • Liver and intrahepatic bile duct: HR 0.31 (0.18–0.49), an estimated 69% lower relative rate.
  • Rectum: HR 0.60 (0.42–0.85), about 40% lower.
  • Lung: HR 0.59 (0.38–0.91), about 41% lower.
  • Prostate: HR 0.48 (0.31–0.74), about 52% lower.
  • Breast: HR 0.64 (0.47–0.88), about 36% lower.

These are relative comparisons of hazard ratios. They do not mean that an individual’s chance of cancer fell by these percentages, and they are not absolute risk reductions. There were only 113 liver cancer cases and 363 rectal cancer cases in the analysis, so some estimates are imprecise and need further study.[1]

Possible explanations: diet and biological markers

One possible explanation is that lower sugar exposure while tastes and eating patterns are developing can shape food preferences later in life. In Oxford WebQ diet questionnaires from UK Biobank, post-rationing cohorts reported 50–100 grams more food per day, scored 1–1.5 points lower on a healthy-diet index, and scored 0.5–1 point lower on a food-diversity index. These data came from 24-hour dietary recalls, up to four per participant, so they offer snapshots rather than a complete record of long-term diet.

A second possible pathway appears in biological markers. Cohorts exposed to rationing through at least 19 months after birth had leukocyte telomere length that was 0.05 standard deviations longer on average. The authors translated this into a difference equivalent to about 2.2 years on a biological-aging marker, based on the annual change measured in UK Biobank. Rationed cohorts also had lower levels of Granzyme B, a marker that may reflect chronic inflammation and immune activation. But a single measurement of telomere length or Granzyme B cannot reveal a person’s actual lifespan or predict future cancer. These are clues to possible pathways, not proven mediators.[1]

What the study does not tell us

First, it does not show that sugar in childhood was the sole cause of later cancer. The end of rationing created a difference in sugar exposure, but individual diets, household habits, smoking, pollution and other health risks can change over decades. The authors adjusted for several factors and ran sensitivity analyses, but an observational natural experiment cannot eliminate every unknown difference.

Second, UK Biobank volunteers are not a perfect representation of the whole population. The researchers reweighted their estimates to reduce healthy-volunteer selection bias, but whether the findings apply to other countries, ancestry groups or today’s food environment remains an open question. Diet was measured through limited recall periods, and the biological markers were measured only once.

What should we take from it?

The study adds to the public-health case for paying attention to excessive added sugar in young children’s diets. But historical rationing should not be treated as a direct prescription for a specific sugar target or as a guarantee of cancer prevention. Current nutrition guidance, a child’s age and health, and advice from a clinician or dietitian all matter separately.[3]

The broader lesson may be that nutrition and food environments at the start of life can have consequences beyond immediate growth. To understand how large those effects are, researchers will need replication across populations, direct measures of diet and continued long-term follow-up.
