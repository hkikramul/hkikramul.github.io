One Year With a Pig Kidney: A Medical Milestone

Biggan Jiggasha · November 24, 2026

Transplanting an animal organ into a human is not a new idea. The harder question is how long the organ can function and how to prevent the immune system from rejecting it. Molly Falco, 58, has reached one year with a genetically edited pig kidney and has not returned to dialysis. This is a major milestone, but it does not yet establish xenotransplantation as routine care.

How did she reach one year?

On November 22, 2025, surgeons at Massachusetts General Hospital transplanted an investigational EGEN-2784 kidney, developed by eGenesis, into Falco. On November 22, 2026, she reached the one-year mark. The hospital and company announced that the kidney was still functioning and that she remained dialysis-free. To their knowledge, this is the longest sustained function of a genetically edited pig kidney in a living human recipient.[1]

A congenital condition diagnosed during childhood had damaged Falco’s kidneys. She began dialysis in 2024. A New York Times report says the transplanted kidney filters waste from her blood, makes urine, and helps balance fluids and minerals. Falco still tires quickly and wears a mask in public to reduce infection risk. She also takes medicines to prevent rejection. These drugs suppress immune function and can increase susceptibility to infection.[2]

What is xenotransplantation?

Xenotransplantation is the transplantation of organs or tissues between different species. Pig organs are being studied because their size and some physiological features are relatively close to those of human organs. But the human immune system can recognize a pig organ as foreign and attack it. Infection and other biological risks also need to be considered.

Gene editing is one way researchers try to reduce these barriers. EGEN-2784 has multiple edits intended to address some causes of immune rejection and biological incompatibility. The New York Times reported that the donor pig had 69 genetic edits, including edits to inactivate porcine retroviruses. These changes are efforts to manage risk; they do not eliminate it.[2]

What have earlier transplants taught researchers?

In 2024, Massachusetts General Hospital transplanted a pig kidney into Richard Slayman. He died 52 days later; the hospital said his death was related to a heart problem, not directly to the transplanted kidney. In a later case, Tim Andrews’s pig kidney functioned for 271 days before it was removed and replaced with a kidney from a human donor. Other recipients had their pig kidneys removed after rejection or declining function.[2]

In a November 2026 announcement, eGenesis and Mass General Brigham said six patients had received EGEN-2784 kidneys under the FDA’s Expanded Access pathway. Expanded Access can permit investigational treatment in special circumstances. It is not general approval or an established treatment available to everyone.[1]

A milestone, not the final answer

A year without dialysis shows that an edited pig kidney can function in a human for an extended period. That gives researchers confidence and a basis for further study. But one patient’s experience cannot establish long-term safety, effectiveness across many patients, the organ’s expected lifespan, or the best balance of immunosuppressive medicines. Infection, rejection, and the possibility of infections crossing from pigs to people all require continued monitoring.

Many people in the United States depend on dialysis, and the wait for a suitable human donor kidney can be long. Xenotransplantation could eventually offer another option. It is not yet ready to replace organ donation: researchers still need evidence from larger clinical studies, long-term follow-up, and regulatory review.

In short: Molly Falco’s year with a pig kidney is a landmark in transplantation research. It is evidence of possibility, not a guarantee of a final solution.
