Is AI Taking Over Mathematics? Faster Discovery and the Human Question

Biggan Jiggasha · November 24, 2026

Some mathematical problems have taken people decades to solve. Now technology companies say that artificial intelligence can produce possible solutions to hundreds of open problems in a short time. But finding an answer quickly is not the same as proving that it is correct or understanding why it works. Recent advances in AI have created major opportunities, difficult verification work and concerns about the future of mathematical research.

This article is an explanatory adaptation of a news feature by Davide Castelvecchi in Nature. It is not a complete or word-for-word translation. It also does not assume that every AI solution discussed in the report has been independently verified and accepted by mathematicians.[1]

Claims of hundreds of solutions, with verification still ahead

According to Nature, on October 6, 2026, OpenAI released more than 700 preprints. The company said they contained solutions to more than 300 important unsolved problems in mathematics. A preprint is a version of a research paper made publicly available, often before publication. Its availability does not by itself mean that the result has passed peer review or been accepted as correct. The distinction between “a solution has been claimed” and “mathematicians have checked and accepted it” matters.

This wave did not come out of nowhere. Earlier AI-assisted claims about problems in group theory and a result on the Navier–Stokes problem, which concerns fluid flow, had already drawn attention. Researchers were struck by the scale of the new claims. AI can combine ideas from different fields and produce a proof or a proof draft faster than a researcher might find one alone. Yet people still need time to check a long argument for a small error, examine its assumptions and understand what the result means.

The Riemann hypothesis: a major claim, careful checking

The most widely discussed example in the report is the Riemann hypothesis. Proposed in the nineteenth century, it is closely connected to deep patterns in the distribution of prime numbers. The report says OpenAI first released a result on a partial form of the hypothesis. Later, Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge said he had found a proof of the full Riemann hypothesis with help from Claude. Soon afterward, Rome-based mathematician Alessandro Masullo announced a separate proof that he had been developing for years.

It is too early to say whether this will become a historic result. Nature reports Oxford mathematician James Maynard as saying that it would be a historic milestone if the proof withstands full checking. That condition is central. For extraordinary mathematical claims, independent scrutiny by other specialists, reconstructing every step of the argument and, where useful, formal proof checking all matter.

Is a mathematician’s job only to find answers?

If an AI can produce in minutes a result that might have taken people decades, what role remains for mathematicians? Many researchers see AI as a powerful assistant. It can search for promising approaches, handle long calculations, test examples or help organize a proof draft. Researchers can then focus on choosing questions, checking assumptions, finding gaps in reasoning and interpreting new results. Used this way, AI could open a new era of mathematical discovery.

But producing a proof and understanding its meaning are different things. If AI generates results much faster than people can read and check them, the store of mathematical results may grow while it becomes harder to understand, teach and use them. Mathematics is not only about what the answer is. It is also about why it is true, which ideas led to it and how it connects to other results.

Open research and uncertainty for early-career mathematicians

Rapid publication of AI-assisted results could also change research culture. Mathematicians often share unfinished ideas and problems in seminars and conversations. If they fear that someone with a powerful model will quickly turn a disclosed idea into a paper, they may hesitate to discuss work in progress. That could weaken collaboration and open exchange. It may also become harder to assess who contributed what when research relies heavily on AI.

The uncertainty is especially sharp for PhD students and early-career researchers. In a UK-based survey discussed by Nature, 89% of students thought AI could weaken their development as mathematicians, compared with 68% of established academics. Another survey found that most graduate students and postdocs expected AI advances to make tenure-track positions harder to obtain. These surveys do not represent every researcher in every country, but they point to real concerns about training, jobs and credit for research.

What does this mean for students in Bangladesh?

For students and early-career researchers in Bangladesh, AI could help with learning mathematics, checking code, generating examples and understanding ideas in new papers. But a model’s confident explanation is not necessarily correct. Foundational mathematics, checking each step of an argument, consulting sources and explaining a proof in one’s own words will become even more important. Educational institutions will need to teach responsible AI use while still evaluating students’ independent reasoning and contributions.

Technology companies, universities, journals and mathematicians will need to decide together how AI assistance should be disclosed, how proofs should be checked, how credit should be shared and how future mathematicians should be trained. If publishing results quickly becomes the only measure of success, deep understanding and collaborative inquiry could lose ground.

AI may change much of what mathematicians do. But people should remain responsible for forming questions, understanding the meaning of proofs, identifying errors and deciding which knowledge is useful to society. The central question is not simply “Can AI do mathematics?” It is how we will check, understand and use the mathematics produced with AI in ways that benefit everyone.
