The Evolution of Mathematics in the Indian Subcontinent

Biggan Jiggasha

The subcontinent in the history of mathematics

In 305, the middle zero indicates that the tens place is empty. To understand the history of that small mark, we have to look at manuscripts, inscriptions, computational rules and astronomical texts together. This history predates modern national borders and draws on Sanskrit works from several regions, their commentaries, teaching traditions and the circulation of ideas beyond the subcontinent. [1,2]

Cords and geometry in the Śulbasūtras

The Śulbasūtras are texts concerned with Vedic ritual. They use geometry in rules for building altars of prescribed shapes and areas. They also record the relation between the square on a rectangle’s diagonal and the squares on its two sides. For sides of three and four units, the relation gives a diagonal of five units. A practical task behind these constructions was to change an altar’s shape while preserving the area prescribed for it. [1,2]

From a placeholder to arithmetic with zero

The Bakhshālī manuscript uses a dot for an empty place. Radiocarbon tests on some of its birch-bark leaves produced different date ranges. Scholars disagree about how those measurements should be used to date the writing and reconstruct the manuscript’s history. In the Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta of 628, Brahmagupta set out rules for calculating with zero and positive and negative numbers. Many of his addition and subtraction rules look familiar, while his treatment of division by zero differs from modern arithmetic. [1,3,4]

Astronomy, commentaries and infinite series

In the Āryabhaṭīya of 499, Aryabhata placed computation alongside astronomy. It includes tables related to the modern sine function and the kuttaka method for certain linear equations with integer solutions. Later, Bhāskara II’s Līlāvatī and Bījagaṇita became important for teaching arithmetic and equations. Commentaries gave readers explanations and worked examples alongside the terse verse of the original texts. [1,2,5]

In Kerala, works associated with Madhava and later mathematicians contain infinite series for trigonometric quantities and for computing π. Adding the first few terms gives an approximation. How that approximation changes as terms are added was also an object of study. Later Kerala works preserved and explained such results. Comparisons with European calculus are possible, though firm documentary evidence of transmission from Kerala to Europe is lacking. [1,2,6]

New connections in modern research

In the colonial era, colleges, examinations and journals changed the institutional setting of mathematical education. Late in that period, the contact between Srinivasa Ramanujan and G. H. Hardy became a well-known episode in the research ties between India and Britain. One question they studied together was how many ways an integer can be written as a sum of positive integers. The distance from altar construction to partitions shows how both the questions and the institutions that support mathematical work change over time. [1,7]
