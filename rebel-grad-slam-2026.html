Practice story

Imagine two nineteen-year-olds in Bangladesh. One began living with her husband at fourteen; the other at eighteen. If we compare whether they have had a baby, are we comparing only their circumstances—or also how long their clocks have been running?

These are hypothetical examples. But the question is real.

My research asks how the timing of living together relates to having had a live birth or being currently pregnant among married adolescents in Bangladesh.

I analysed the 2022 Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey. For the descriptive analysis, I studied 2,449 ever-married adolescents aged fifteen to nineteen. About six in ten had already had a live birth or were pregnant at the time of the survey. That figure describes ever-married adolescents—not all adolescent girls in Bangladesh.

The next question was what patterns sit behind that number. Among 1,601 currently married respondents with the information needed for our model, I examined cohabitation timing alongside current age, household circumstances, and partner characteristics.

Because this is a national survey, some respondents represent more people than others. I accounted for those sampling differences and checked the calculations independently in R and Python.

Here is the important lesson: when current age was included, the estimated timing associations changed substantially. Later cohabitation was associated with lower odds of the outcome. But someone who began living with her husband recently has also had less time for childbearing.

So we cannot turn that association into a promise about what a marriage policy would achieve. The regression findings also remain provisional while we confirm the weighting for the longer questionnaire.

My contribution is making those distinctions visible: who the estimate describes, how the survey was analysed, and what the evidence can support.

The prevalence finding motivates closer attention to reproductive-health care for married adolescents. The timing analysis reminds us that responsible statistics must explain the clock behind the number.

A national survey can show us an important pattern. Careful interpretation tells us what that pattern actually means.

Practice in short sessions

Target 2:40–2:50, measured aloud. Two timed runs without notes, then one correction. Shorten wording instead of speaking faster. Ask a non-statistician to identify the question, finding and contribution. The story is a draft; adapt it to your own voice.

  1. 0:00–0:25: hypothetical comparison and research question.
  2. 0:25–0:55: population and main finding.
  3. 0:55–1:35: analysis and contribution.
  4. 1:35–2:15: interpretation and uncertainty.
  5. 2:15–2:50: relevance and closing.
